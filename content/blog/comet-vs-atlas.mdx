Two AI labs, two new browsers, one bet: that the browser itself — not just the search box — is the next place people ask questions and get things done. Perplexity shipped Comet first. OpenAI answered with Atlas. Both are trying to answer the same question in a different accent: what does browsing look like once an AI is doing some of the driving? If you're deciding between them, or wondering whether either is worth leaving Chrome for, here's the direct, unsponsored comparison.

## Comet vs Atlas: the short answer

Comet and Atlas each inherited their parent's obsession, and that's the fastest way to understand them. Comet browses like a research engine that shows its work. Atlas browses like a conversation with an agent that can also act on your behalf. Pick based on which AI you already trust with real work.

- **Comet (Perplexity)** treats the page you're on like a source to be cited. Ask it something and it tends to answer the way Perplexity search does — with receipts attached.
- **Atlas (OpenAI)** treats the page you're on like a conversation. ChatGPT is woven directly into the browsing session, and it leans further into taking actions, not just answering questions.

Neither one is finished. Both are early releases from labs that ship fast and iterate in public, so specifics — pricing, feature availability, extension support — can change week to week. Treat any comparison you read today, including this one, as a snapshot. Check each product's own release notes before you build a workflow around either one.

## What Comet does best

Comet's edge is research you can actually verify. Perplexity built its search product around showing sources instead of just asserting answers, and Comet carries that habit into the browser itself.

- Ask it a question mid-browse and it tends to answer with citations rather than a flat statement — useful when you're fact-checking, comparing claims across sites, or writing something that needs a paper trail.
- It fits workflows where "where did that come from" is a real question: competitive research, due diligence, sourcing a quote, checking a number before you repeat it publicly.
- Multi-tab research sessions — the kind where you have a dozen tabs open and are trying to synthesize them — are closer to Comet's home turf than most general-purpose assistants, because summarizing-with-sources is what Perplexity has optimized for from day one.
- If you already default to Perplexity over Google for search, Comet is the natural extension of a habit you've already built. Our full [Comet browser](/blog/comet-browser) breakdown covers what's changed since launch.

Where it's weaker, qualitatively: the more agentic tasks — filling out forms, executing multi-step actions across sites — are newer and less proven than Comet's citation-first search experience. Confirm current capabilities directly before you lean on it for anything with real stakes attached.

## What Atlas does best

Atlas leans into the fact that ChatGPT is already the tool people open dozens of times a day. Instead of asking you to bring your question to a new product, Atlas puts ChatGPT inside the browsing surface you're already using.

- The pull here is ecosystem as much as interface. ChatGPT reported 800 million weekly active users as of October 2025, per TechCrunch — an enormous existing habit that Atlas can plug straight into rather than compete against.
- It suits people who already think "ask ChatGPT to do this" and want that instinct to extend from a chat window into a full browsing session, including agent-style actions taken on your behalf.
- Early coverage of Atlas has focused on its agent mode — letting it click through multi-step tasks like filling forms or booking something — which is a different bet than Comet's citation-first approach and, by nature, a harder one to get right consistently.
- If your work already routes through ChatGPT for drafting, planning, or coding, Atlas removes a context switch instead of adding a new tool to learn. See our [ChatGPT Atlas](/blog/chatgpt-atlas) deep dive for the specifics.

Where it's weaker, qualitatively: citing sources isn't as central to ChatGPT's DNA as it is to Perplexity's. If verifiable sourcing is your top priority, don't assume Atlas matches Comet there by default — check what the current version actually surfaces before you rely on it.

Side by side, the philosophical split looks like this:

| | Comet (Perplexity) | Atlas (OpenAI) |
|---|---|---|
| Parent engine | Perplexity | ChatGPT |
| Core philosophy | Answer-engine-first | Assistant-first |
| Citations posture | Central; sourcing is the default habit | Present but secondary — verify current behavior |
| Agent capabilities posture | Emerging, layered onto a search-first product | A stated focus, layered onto a chat-first product |
| Maturity | Early, actively iterating | Early, actively iterating |
| Pick it if | You want a browser that argues like a researcher | You want your ChatGPT habit extended into browsing |

## Comet vs Chrome (and Atlas vs Chrome)

Comet vs Chrome isn't really a fair fight yet, and neither is Atlas vs Chrome — not because the challengers are weak, but because Chrome has had twenty years and they've had months. Chrome still wins on maturity, extensions, and plain stability. Comet and Atlas win on AI that's built into the browsing itself instead of bolted on. And Chrome is closing that gap from its own side, which makes this a moving target rather than a settled score.

- Chrome has two decades of extension ecosystem, enterprise policy support, and the unglamorous virtue of not crashing on your fortieth open tab. That's a real reason most people won't rip it out on day one.
- What Comet and Atlas offer that Chrome doesn't yet, natively, is AI woven into the browsing session — no separate window, no copy-pasting a URL into a chat tab to ask about the page you're already on.
- Google isn't standing still here. It's building Gemini directly into Chrome, which means the "AI-native browser" edge Comet and Atlas hold today is temporary unless they keep moving faster than the incumbent.
- Extension compatibility is a genuinely open question for both challengers, not a settled one. Comet and Atlas share Chromium's lineage, which is a reason for optimism — but lineage isn't a guarantee. Check current docs before assuming your extension list travels over untouched.

None of that makes the comparison pointless — it just means the right answer today might not be the right answer in six months.

## Should you switch today?

Try one as a secondary browser before it becomes your daily driver. Only replace Chrome outright if AI-native workflows are core to your job, not just a nice-to-have.

- Install Comet or Atlas alongside Chrome, not instead of it, and run a real workweek through it before deciding anything.
- Researchers, analysts, and writers who live in "find it, verify it, cite it" mode should trial Comet first.
- Anyone already living inside ChatGPT for drafting, planning, or coding should trial Atlas first.
- If you're evaluating this for a team rather than yourself, pilot with a handful of people first — extension gaps and workflow quirks surface fast in daily use, and they're cheaper to find with five users than five hundred.
- If your browser just needs to work — banking, enterprise tools, one extension you can't lose — stay on Chrome until one of these has logged a lot more miles. Our broader [best AI browser](/blog/best-ai-browser) roundup covers the rest of the field if neither of these is the right fit.
