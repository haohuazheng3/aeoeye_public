## What is SGE (Search Generative Experience)?

SGE stands for Search Generative Experience — Google's experimental AI feature that generated a written summary from web sources instead of a plain list of links, launched as an opt-in test inside Search Labs. As of this writing, SGE no longer exists under that name: it evolved into AI Overviews, then into the more conversational AI Mode. So if you searched \"what is SGE\" hoping to find a current Google feature, the honest answer is that you're really asking about the early version of what Google now ships by default.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A lot of \"SGE optimization\" advice floating around treats SGE like a fixed target — a checklist to nail once and move on. It never was. SGE was a codename for an experiment, not a destination. Google tests format changes constantly under Labs branding, then folds the ones that work into mainstream Search under a new name. Chasing the label instead of the underlying shift is how you end up optimizing for something that's already been renamed twice.

What SGE actually did, while it existed under that name:

- Generated a written answer synthesized from several web sources, not just one
- Displayed that answer above the traditional list of organic results
- Cited a short, curated list of sources alongside the summary
- Stayed limited to opt-in Search Labs users, mostly in the US, on a subset of queries

SGE was one specific implementation of a broader shift often called [generative search](/blog/generative-search) — search engines that generate an answer instead of only ranking pages. Google wasn't first to try this and won't be the last, but its version is the one most people mean when they ask \"what is SGE.\"

## From SGE to AI Overviews to AI Mode

Google's generative search moved through three distinct stages — SGE the experiment, AI Overviews as the default experience, and AI Mode as a fully conversational tab — each one wider in audience and deeper in capability than the last.

**SGE (the experiment).** Opt-in only, through Search Labs. Limited to a subset of queries, mostly English-language, mostly US users. Its job was to test the format and collect feedback, not to be the finished product.

**AI Overviews (the rollout).** This is where the \"SGE\" name got retired. The AI-generated summary graduated out of Labs and became a default part of regular search results for eligible queries — no opt-in required — and expanded to more countries and languages over time. For a large share of searchers, this is simply what Google looks like now.

**AI Mode (the conversation).** As of this writing, the newest stage adds a dedicated, chat-style tab inside Search. Instead of a single summary, you can ask follow-up questions, refine what you meant, and work through multi-part questions in one thread — closer to talking with an AI assistant than scanning a results page. It's the clearest sign Google is competing directly with conversational tools, not just bolting a widget onto its existing product. Curious how that stacks up against a dedicated chat product? [Our ChatGPT vs. Google comparison](/blog/chatgpt-vs-google) breaks down where each one wins.

The pattern is worth remembering: Google ships a limited, renamable experiment, learns from it, then expands under new branding. If you want to find today's version of \"SGE,\" watch the edges of AI Mode — that's where the next round of testing is happening right now.

## How Google's generative search works

Google's AI search isn't a replacement for its index — it's a summarization layer sitting on top of it. The system still crawls and ranks pages the way it always has; the difference is what happens after ranking.

Roughly, here's the sequence:

1. Google retrieves the pages it considers most relevant, using the same index and ranking systems behind classic search
2. A language model reads the top-ranking pages and extracts the key facts
3. It drafts a synthesized answer, often blending information from several sources into one paragraph
4. It attaches a short list of citation links back to the sources it drew from
5. Regular organic results still appear on the page, usually below or beside the generated answer

That last point gets missed a lot: generative search didn't delete the ten blue links, it just demoted them below an AI-written summary for a growing share of queries. For the fuller picture of how this fits alongside ChatGPT, Perplexity, and other AI answer engines, see our breakdown of [what AI search is](/blog/what-is-ai-search).

This retrieve-then-synthesize process is exactly why structure matters so much now. A model can only cite what it can confidently extract — vague, meandering pages don't make it into the summary, no matter how well they'd rank in classic search.

Here's how the three formats actually compare, side by side:

| Aspect | Classic Google Results | SGE | AI Overviews & AI Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Format | Ranked list of ten blue links | AI-written summary above results (opt-in, Search Labs only) | AI-written summary built into default results; AI Mode adds a full conversational thread |
| What it shows | Page titles, URLs, and short text snippets | A synthesized paragraph answer plus a handful of source links | A synthesized answer with expandable sources; AI Mode adds multi-turn, follow-up answers |
| Where links appear | Every single result is a clickable link | A small citation list next to or below the summary | A small citation list; AI Mode links appear inline as the conversation continues |
| User interaction | Scan titles, compare snippets, click through | Read the summary first, click a source only if needed | Read, click a source, or ask a follow-up without leaving the page |

## What SGE/AI Overviews mean for your traffic

The blunt version: AI-generated answers increasingly let people get what they need without clicking anything, so zero-click search keeps growing, and visibility is shifting from \"where do I rank\" to \"does the AI answer actually mention me.\"

This isn't speculation. Independent analyses of real Google searches have consistently found that people click through to traditional results less often when an AI summary sits on top, and are more likely to end a search without clicking at all. That's not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to stop measuring success by rank alone.

Here's what's actually changing:

- Ranking #1 without being cited in the AI answer is now a real, mostly invisible form of lost traffic
- Being quoted or summarized inside an AI Overview functions like a new \"position zero\" — sometimes with a click, often without one
- Brand mentions inside an AI answer still build awareness and trust even when nobody clicks through
- The pages that win citations tend to be the ones that state a clear, direct answer early, not the ones that simply rank highest overall

We'd push back on the \"SEO is dead\" framing you'll see elsewhere. It's not dead — the scoreboard changed. Being ranked and being cited used to be almost the same thing. Now they're two different jobs, and most sites are only working on one of them.

## How to show up in Google's AI search

You earn a place in AI Overviews and AI Mode the same way you'd earn a place in any AI answer engine: by being clearly indexed, cleanly structured, and easy to quote with confidence. Google's AI can't cite what it can't confidently extract.

A few things that actually move the needle:

- **Get the fundamentals right first.** AI Overviews pull from the same index as regular search — if you're not indexed and crawlable, you're not eligible, full stop.
- **Answer the question in the first sentence or two of a section**, not buried in paragraph five. That's the exact shape a model is trying to lift.
- **Use real structure**: headers phrased the way people actually ask questions, short paragraphs, numbered steps, comparison tables. Structure isn't decoration — it's what makes a page machine-parseable.
- **Add schema markup** — FAQPage, Article, HowTo, and similar — so systems don't have to guess what a page is about. Google's own [Search Central documentation](https://developers.google.com/search) covers the structured data types it recognizes.
- **Keep entities unambiguous.** State your brand name, product name, and specific claims plainly and consistently — don't make a model infer them from context.
- **Earn citations elsewhere.** Generative search tends to lean on sources that already read as trustworthy across the web, so being referenced on other sites strengthens your odds of being the one selected.

Rankings alone won't answer that — the only way to know for certain is to check what Google's AI is actually saying when someone asks about your brand or your category.

You can spot-check this by hand: run your target queries through AI Mode and see what comes back. It works, but it's slow, and answers shift depending on query phrasing, session, and location, so a handful of manual searches won't give you the full picture.
