## What is a SERP?

A SERP (search engine results page) is the page a search engine returns after you run a query. That's the textbook definition. What most explanations leave out: a SERP used to be a simple, static list of ten blue links, and today it's closer to a control panel — ads, answer boxes, image carousels, AI-generated summaries, and links, all competing for the same few inches of screen.

You'll see "SERP" used constantly in SEO conversations — SERP position, SERP features, SERP volatility. It all points to the same thing: what shows up on that results page, and where.

Most SEO tools — rank trackers, dashboards, reporting software — are built around monitoring exactly this: what a specific SERP looks like for a specific query, and how it shifts over time. That's "SERP tracking," and it's a large chunk of what SEO software actually does day to day.

Here's the part that actually matters for your strategy: the "results" in SERP no longer just mean links to other websites. Increasingly, the SERP *is* the answer. A search engine would rather resolve your query right there than send you somewhere else. That single shift is why search optimization today looks nothing like it did a decade ago — and why holding the #1 organic spot isn't the win it used to be.

## What's on a modern SERP

A modern SERP is layered, and most of those layers didn't exist ten years ago. Depending on the query, a single results page can include:

- **Organic results** — the unpaid listings ranked by relevance and authority; still the backbone of the page
- **Paid ads** — sponsored listings, typically stacked above the organic results and sometimes below them too
- **Featured snippets** — a boxed answer extracted from one page, displayed above position one
- **People Also Ask (PAA)** — expandable related questions that reveal more the further you click
- **AI Overviews** — AI-generated summaries synthesized from multiple sources, usually anchored at the top of the page
- **Local packs** — a map plus a short list of nearby businesses, triggered by location-specific searches
- **Knowledge panels** — sidebar boxes of structured facts about a person, place, or entity

Not every query pulls in every feature. A narrow, technical B2B search might still render as a plain list of links. A broad, popular query — "best running shoes," "what is inflation" — can stack several of these features before a single organic link even appears on screen.

Mobile SERPs compress this even further. Less screen space means features like AI Overviews and local packs take up an even larger share of what's visible before any scrolling happens, which is worth remembering if you're only ever checking rankings on desktop.

## The SERP is not just ten blue links anymore

Sit with this for a second: every feature added to the SERP is space that organic links used to own outright. Featured snippets, PAA boxes, and AI Overviews don't sit politely beside the traditional results — they sit *on top of* them, pushing the ten blue links further down the page and further from the click.

This is, in our view, the most underrated shift in modern search. People still talk about "ranking #1" as if it's the finish line. But position one on a page topped by an AI Overview isn't the prize it was when that phrase got coined. Here's how the major pieces break down:

| SERP element | What it is | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Organic results | Unpaid listings ranked by relevance and authority | Still worth earning, but no longer a guarantee of visibility, even at #1 |
| Paid ads | Listings purchased through the search engine's ad platform | Compete for the same attention as organic results, often positioned above them |
| Featured snippet | One extracted answer, displayed above position 1 | Can win the click even from position 2–5, if you're the cited source |
| People Also Ask | Expandable box of related questions | A map of the exact phrasing people search — and a chance to get quoted mid-page |
| AI Overview | AI-generated synthesis pulled from multiple sources | Can fully answer the query on the page itself, removing the need to click at all |
| Local pack | Map plus a short list of nearby businesses | Owns the top of the page for local intent, with no organic link required |
| Knowledge panel | Structured facts about an entity | A signal the search engine already recognizes who you are |

The pattern repeats in every row: visibility now depends on whether you show up *inside* one of these boxes, not just on whether you rank.

## Why the SERP matters for your strategy

Where you appear on the SERP decides whether anyone actually sees your site — not just whether you technically rank for the term. That distinction is where a lot of SEO strategy still goes wrong: chasing position instead of presence.

Checking the SERP for a target keyword before you write anything is still one of the highest-leverage habits in SEO. It tells you which features are already active, who currently holds them, and whether the query is even worth the effort — information no keyword tool alone can give you.

Picture two outcomes for the same keyword:

- **Ranking #1 organically**, buried beneath an AI Overview, a PAA block, and a run of ads — technically first, practically invisible
- **Ranking further down the page**, but cited as the source inside the featured snippet or the AI Overview — visible before the user scrolls at all

The second outcome frequently wins. A feature placement or an AI citation can out-perform a top organic slot because it's positioned higher on the page and answers the query directly, without asking the user to click through first.

This is also why SERP volatility gets tracked so closely. A page that held a snippet last month and lost it isn't necessarily ranking worse — the feature itself may have disappeared, changed shape, or gone to a competitor. Watching only your organic position misses that entirely.

None of this replaces the fundamentals, though. [Crawling](/blog/what-is-crawling) and [indexing](/blog/what-is-indexing) — the two steps that have to happen before your page can rank, get featured, or get cited anywhere — still come first. No page lands in a snippet, a PAA box, or an AI Overview without being crawled and indexed beforehand.

The basics haven't gotten less important. They've gotten less sufficient on their own.

## The SERP in the AI search era

The biggest recent shift: AI Overviews turn the SERP itself into the answer, so a growing share of searches end without a single click to any website. The search engine generates a synthesized response right there on the results page, pulling from several sources at once — and plenty of users read that response and move on without visiting anyone's site. That's the zero-click pattern, and it's growing, not shrinking.

For publishers and brands, that's an uncomfortable trade: you can be the uncredited source behind an AI Overview and see none of the traffic that used to come with ranking well. Visibility and traffic have started to decouple, and a lot of SEO reporting hasn't caught up to that yet.

But here's what most SERP explainers leave out, and it's arguably the bigger story: this is only half the shift.

Step off Google entirely, onto ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini, and there's no SERP to speak of. No ten links, no stack of competing boxes, no snippet fighting an ad for attention — just one conversational answer. Either your brand is part of that answer, or it effectively doesn't exist for that query. This is the core of what we mean by [AI search](/blog/what-is-ai-search): a move away from *serving a ranked list* and toward *generating a single response*.

That changes the target. Optimizing to rank well on a traditional SERP and optimizing to be cited inside an AI-generated answer are related skills, but they aren't the same skill. The second one is what [ranking in AI Overviews](/blog/how-to-rank-in-ai-overviews) actually demands: answers written clearly and structured for extraction, not just for ranking algorithms.
