Law firms get recommended by AI engines the way they win local search: an exact Google Business Profile, bar and directory records that match it, real reviews, and practice-area pages that answer the client's question in the first two sentences. Nobody publishes an AI ranking formula for lawyers, so the work is being consistent and verifiable everywhere an engine reads.

This guide rests on public documentation and rule texts as of October 2026. We have not run legal marketing campaigns, and it is not legal advice: check your own bar's rules.

## How do AI engines pick a law firm to recommend?

No AI company publishes how it chooses firms for a local legal question, so plan around the sources an engine can read about you. The documented parts are narrow: Google says a page must be [indexed and eligible for a snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) to appear as a link in AI Overviews or AI Mode, and lists keeping your Business Profile information current among its checks.

OpenAI says [OAI-SearchBot surfaces sites in ChatGPT search](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots), and sites that opt out of it are not shown in ChatGPT search answers.

Six places shape what an engine can say about a firm:

| Source | What it tells an engine | Your control |
|---|---|---|
| Firm website | Practice areas, jurisdictions, attorneys, plain answers | Full |
| Google Business Profile | Name, address, category, hours, reviews | Mostly |
| State bar record | License status; some bars also show discipline history | None, but keep your details current |
| Legal directories | Third-party confirmation of what you do | Partial |
| Client reviews | How clients describe the work | Indirect |
| Local press, associations | Independent mentions of the firm | Earned |

Our read: an engine names a firm with confidence when these sources agree, and hedges or skips it when they don't. Making them agree is [entity SEO](/blog/what-is-entity-seo).

## Why should law firms care about AI answers now?

Because prospective clients already ask chatbots about their legal problem before they call anyone. Pew found that [49% of U.S. adults used AI chatbots in February 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/americans-and-ai-2026-chatbots-smart-devices-and-views-on-impact/), up from 33% in 2024, and about four in ten used them to search for information.

Legal trouble shows the pattern clearly. A UK study by JUSTICE and the Administrative Fairness Lab surveyed 3,287 people in December 2025 to January 2026: [233 of the 1,428 with a recent legal dispute (16.3%) had used a chatbot](https://www.justice.org.uk/reports/what-ai-chatbots-can-teach-us-about-unmet-legal-needs) for help, information or advice. The share was 26% among 18-to-24-year-olds and 10% among 55-to-64-year-olds. Some used it to translate legalese or to check their own lawyer's work.

Our position: that is the research phase, not the hiring phase. The page that explains what happens after an arrest or a crash is where a firm first gets named; the Business Profile and reviews are where it gets chosen. Both need work.

Clicks are thinning too. In Pew's March 2025 browsing data, people [clicked a result link in 8% of visits to Google pages with an AI summary, against 15% without](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), and clicked a link inside the summary in 1%. When we looked at AEOeye's own audit records (127 audits, June to September 2026, self-selected brands), a brand that was named and ranked came first in 71.4% of 147 ranked mentions. Small and skewed, but it points the same way: [being named at all is the hard part](/blog/ai-brand-recommendation-measurement).

## What is the GEO playbook for a law firm?

Seven jobs, in this order: first fix the places that decide who you are, then the pages that show what you know. The first three cost little and need no new writing.

1. **Make the Google Business Profile exact.** Google wants your [real-world name as used on your storefront, website and stationery](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en), with no taglines or keywords, a precise street address (no P.O. boxes) and as few, specific categories as possible. Lawyers are "individual practitioners": when several lawyers share a location, the firm has its own profile and each lawyer's carries only the lawyer's name. A practitioner shouldn't open several profiles to cover specializations.
2. **Make the bar record and directories match.** Compare name, address, phone and practice areas across your bar listing, directories and profile. State bars such as [California's publish a public attorney profile](https://apps.calbar.ca.gov/attorney/LicenseeSearch/QuickSearch) with license status and discipline records. Avvo and Justia both blocked our automated fetch today, so we can't describe their claim process: check each one yourself.
3. **Let the crawlers in.** OpenAI says allowing OAI-SearchBot and [disallowing GPTBot is a supported setup](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots), and robots.txt changes take about 24 hours to register. In [AEOeye's crawler access study](/resources/ai-crawler-access-study-2026-10.json) (October 2026), 37 of 384 sites blocked at least one AI retrieval bot, and 60 of 375 homepages challenged our crawler. Law firms weren't a category in that sample, so check your robots.txt, security plugin and CDN rules for yourself.
4. **Write practice-area pages that answer first.** Open with the answer to the client's actual question, then jurisdiction, deadlines, process, how fees work and when to call. Cite the statute or court page. Google says YMYL content must be [highly accurate and consistent with expert consensus](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content), so an attorney reviews every page.
5. **Show the real lawyers.** One bio per attorney: name, bar admissions, practice focus, a link to the bar profile. The same Google guidance asks whether it is clear who created the content.
6. **Add structured data, modestly.** Schema.org [deprecates the Attorney type](https://schema.org/Attorney) in favor of LegalService. Google asks for the [most specific LocalBusiness subtype](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business), with name and address required. It also says no special markup is needed for AI features, so keep it to what is visible on the page.
7. **Cite facts.** In the [GEO research paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735), adding relevant statistics helped most for "Law & Government" queries. That was a generic benchmark, not firm recommendations, but it is a reason to quote the data behind your answers.

## What do the advertising rules change?

They change what you may claim, not what engines can read. Rule 7.1 prohibits false or misleading communications about a lawyer's services, and [D.C.'s version adds](https://www.dcbar.org/for-lawyers/legal-ethics/rules-of-professional-conduct/information-about-legal-services/communications-concerning-a-lawyer%E2%80%99s-services) any assertion that cannot be substantiated. State wording differs, so read yours.

Three consequences for AI-era content:

- **Skip "best lawyer" copy.** D.C.'s comments say comparisons with other lawyers are misleading if they can't be substantiated. An engine can't verify the claim either, so it earns you nothing.
- **Qualify results and endorsements.** The same comments warn that damage awards, verdict records and client endorsements, unless suitably qualified, can create unjustified expectations.
- **Don't answer reviews with case details.** Google says reviews must reflect [genuine experiences and bans incentives](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122), and the FTC's [reviews rule](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers), effective October 21, 2024, targets fake reviews and review suppression. ABA Formal Opinion 496, [as summarized by Louisiana Legal Ethics](https://lalegalethics.org/aba-issues-formal-opinion-on-responding-to-negative-online-reviews/), says a negative review is not a controversy that permits disclosing client confidences, so lawyers should avoid responding.

## What do law firms get wrong?

Most firms fail on consistency and specificity, not on missing "AI tricks". Five repeat offenders:

- **One thin page per practice area,** with no jurisdiction, no deadlines and no answer an engine could quote.
- **Three versions of the firm name** across the website, Business Profile and directories, which reads as three businesses.
- **Several Business Profiles** for one lawyer, one per specialty. Google's guidance says not to.
- **Paid or pressured reviews,** which break Google's policy and risk the FTC rule.
- **Security tooling that blocks every bot,** so retrieval crawlers never see the site.

We also disagree with agencies that sell "AI SEO for lawyers" as a separate discipline. Almost all of it is local SEO, reputation and plain-answer content done carefully, and nobody can guarantee an AI recommendation.

## How do you measure whether AI recommends your firm?

Ask the engines what your clients ask, then count how often your firm appears. Build 10 to 20 prompts from practice area, city and situation, such as "who handles a first-offense DUI in Tucson?" or "do I need a lawyer after a rear-end collision?"

1. Run each prompt in ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Mode, Perplexity and Claude, noting where you asked from.
2. Record which firms are named, how your firm is described and which sources are cited.
3. Repeat each prompt several times. SparkToro's study of 2,961 runs found [under a 1-in-100 chance of the same brand list twice](https://sparktoro.com/blog/new-research-ais-are-highly-inconsistent-when-recommending-brands-or-products-marketers-should-take-care-when-tracking-ai-visibility/), so one answer proves nothing.
4. Repeat monthly and log it next to calls and intake. The full method is in [measuring AI visibility](/blog/measuring-ai-visibility).
