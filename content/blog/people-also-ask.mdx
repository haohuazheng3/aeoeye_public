People Also Ask has sat in the middle of Google's results for years, and most SEOs still treat it as a curiosity — a box to skim for topic ideas, not a feature worth taking seriously. That's an expensive mistake now. People Also Ask (PAA) was the answer-engine era arriving in disguise, years early: it trained searchers to expect a direct answer instead of a list of blue links, and it trained Google to extract that answer from ordinary web pages — the exact skill that now decides whether ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or Google's AI Overviews cite you. If you've been half-ignoring PAA, you've been skipping the one place in SEO that hands you fast, visible, pass/fail feedback on whether your writing is shaped like an answer at all. This is where you should have been practicing for [answer engine optimization](/blog/what-is-answer-engine-optimization) all along.

Here's what the box actually is, why it beats its click-through rate, how to find the question keywords that feed it, and how to turn PAA wins into AI citations.

## What is People Also Ask?

People Also Ask is the expandable box of related questions Google inserts into search results, usually a few slots below the top organic listings. Click a question and it drops open into a short answer excerpted from a specific page, with a link attached — and the box doesn't stop there.

Expand one question and the box inserts new ones beneath it, pulled from the same topic cluster. Keep clicking and you can pull dozens of related questions out of a single search. That behavior isn't decorative — it's Google showing you, in real time, which question phrasings it has already matched to real search demand.

That's the part worth sitting with: PAA questions aren't invented by a copywriter guessing what people might ask. They map to actual query patterns — the exact words, order, and framing real searchers used often enough for Google to notice. A keyword tool gives you a topic and a volume estimate; PAA gives you the sentence.

## Why PAA matters more than its click-through rate

PAA's click-through rate looks weak because that's the whole design goal — it answers the question right there on the results page, so plenty of searchers never click through. That's why so many SEOs write it off as “zero-click” and move on. That read stops one step too early. That's the tension at the core of people also ask SEO: you're not optimizing to be clicked, you're optimizing to be extracted.

Getting a passage into a PAA slot isn't a traffic play. It's Google looking at a specific paragraph on your page, deciding it cleanly answers a real question, and lifting it out as the answer. Losing the click isn't the cost of that; the extraction is the reward. Owning a slot is proof your content is [quotable by a machine](/answers/what-makes-content-quotable-by-ai) — structured and answered plainly enough that an algorithm was comfortable detaching it from your page and presenting it as the answer on its own.

Think of PAA less as a traffic channel and more as a certification program. Every slot you win is Google certifying, in public, that a specific passage on your site meets the bar for “extractable answer.” That's not a consolation prize for missing the click — it's the exact bar AI answer engines now apply when choosing what to cite, which means SEOs who spent years chasing PAA slots have been unknowingly stress-testing their content against tomorrow's selection criteria.

## How to find question keywords

The fastest way to find question keywords is to mine sources where real people already asked them, instead of guessing. Four places consistently outperform brainstorming:

- **The PAA box itself, expanded recursively.** Search your topic, click every question that appears, then keep clicking the new ones that load underneath. The fifth and sixth layer of questions are usually more specific — and more useful — than the first.
- **Autocomplete.** Type your core topic into the search bar alone, then again with “why,” “how,” “can,” “does,” and “is” in front of it. Autocomplete surfaces the exact fragments people finish typing most often.
- **Your own Search Console data.** Filter your query report for anything phrased as a question — these are searches you're already partially ranking for, which makes them the cheapest wins available.
- **Community threads.** Reddit and Quora surface questions in messy, unfiltered human phrasing — often better raw material than any keyword tool, because nobody optimized the wording before typing it.

On top of those four, a whole category of question-research tools exists that scrapes PAA and autocomplete data at scale into exportable question maps. They're useful for volume and coverage, but they're not a content strategy on their own — most of that category's output is a spreadsheet of questions with no point of view, which is exactly why this SERP is full of thin, list-only pages instead of anyone explaining how to actually win.

## How to win a PAA slot

Winning a PAA slot comes down to structure, not luck: one real question per heading, phrased exactly as a user would ask it, answered in the first 40-60 words before you go anywhere near the nuance. Extraction — Google's or any answer engine's — favors a self-contained passage near the top of a section, not a well-reasoned point buried in paragraph four.

The full recipe:

- **One question per H2 or H3**, in the user's exact phrasing — not a rephrased, SEO-flavored version of it.
- **A 40-60 word answer-first opening** that fully resolves the question, then supporting depth, caveats, and examples for readers who want more.
- **FAQ schema** on pages with genuine Q&A content — it gives Google (and other crawlers) explicit, structured confirmation of which passage answers which question.
- **Target pages that already rank page 1-2** for the broader topic first. PAA doesn't pull from page six — it pulls from pages Google already half-trusts. Existing rankings are the raw material, not a nice-to-have.

The connection between these moves and AI citation isn't a coincidence. It's the same underlying skill, tested by two different graders:

| PAA optimization move | Why it works for PAA | How it pays off in AI answers |
|---|---|---|
| Answer in the first 40-60 words | Extraction favors a self-contained passage near the top | AI engines pull the same kind of passage into a synthesized answer |
| Phrase headings as literal questions | PAA matches question-to-question, not question-to-topic | Chat interfaces are a stream of literal questions — matching phrasing raises retrieval odds |
| Add FAQ schema to genuine Q&A content | Gives Google explicit, structured confirmation of the pairing | The same structured data is often what AI crawlers parse first |
| Target topics you already rank page 1-2 for | PAA pulls from pages Google already trusts | AI engines lean on similar authority signals — trusted pages get cited more |
| Keep one clean answer per section | Mixed intent doesn't extract into a single passage | AI answers need one clean claim per citation; ambiguous sections get skipped |

## From PAA to AI answers

An AI Overview is, structurally, a synthesized PAA box: instead of one question and one extracted passage, it blends several questions' worth of answers into a paragraph with citations attached. Learning to [appear in Google's AI Overviews](/answers/how-to-appear-in-google-ai-overviews) means learning the same extraction logic, just applied at a larger scale.

ChatGPT's answer to a buyer's question is a PAA box with a personality. There's no visible list of expandable questions, no citations shown by default in most interfaces — but underneath, it's still selecting a passage that resolves the question cleanly and presenting it as the answer. If your content already wins PAA slots, you're not starting from zero with AI citations. You're already past the hardest part: writing content shaped like an answer instead of shaped like an article.

The one thing PAA can't tell you is whether it's working across every engine your buyers actually use. Winning a Google PAA slot proves Google trusts a specific passage — it doesn't confirm ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or Claude are citing you when someone asks the buying question directly. Those are separate systems pulling from overlapping but not identical signals, and the only way to know for sure is to check the outputs themselves.
