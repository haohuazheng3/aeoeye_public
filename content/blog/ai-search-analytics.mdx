AI search analytics is most useful when it keeps three kinds of evidence apart: what an AI answer said, which sources it cited, and what people did after visiting your site. Treat those as three linked datasets, not as ingredients in one opaque “visibility score.” This makes changes explainable, lets teams audit the underlying observations, and prevents a rise in mentions from being mistaken for a rise in revenue.

## What should AI search analytics measure first?

Start with answer observations. Record whether your brand appears, how it is described, whether the answer recommends it, and whether the wording is accurate. Then record cited-source evidence, such as the URLs an engine displayed or relied on. Finally, connect eligible visits and conversions in your analytics system.

These layers answer different questions:

- **Answer layer:** Can a buyer find or recognize the brand in an AI response?
- **Citation layer:** Does the engine cite the brand’s pages or other sources when discussing it?
- **Business layer:** Do identifiable visitors arrive and complete a useful action?

An answer can mention a brand without citing its website. A page can be cited without producing a click. A click can occur without a measurable conversion. Keeping these statements separate is the foundation of trustworthy reporting. For a practical introduction, see [measuring AI visibility](/blog/measuring-ai-visibility).

## How is AI search analytics different from AI referral analytics?

AI referral analytics begins with a web visit and asks which referrer, campaign, or landing page sent it. AI search analytics begins earlier, with an observed answer or citation, whether or not anyone clicked. Referral data is downstream evidence; it cannot prove how often a model mentioned you when no click occurred.

For example, a user may read a recommendation in ChatGPT, remember the brand, and later type the domain directly. GA4 may report direct traffic, while an answer observation explains the original influence. Conversely, a visible citation may generate no visit because the user trusted the answer, used a marketplace, or stopped researching.

Use both systems, but do not merge their denominators. A referral rate is visits divided by sessions or users. A mention rate is answers containing a mention divided by eligible answer runs. They are not interchangeable.

## What fields belong in an AI search analytics record?

Store one record per prompt-engine run, with enough context to reproduce the observation. At minimum, include:

| Field | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Prompt | The exact buyer question, including wording and intent |
| Engine | ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google AI, or another system |
| Run ID | A unique identifier for this execution |
| Date and time | When the observation was captured |
| Geography | Country, market, language, and device context when relevant |
| Answer | The captured response or a stable excerpt with a secure archive link |
| Mention | Whether the brand appeared, and its position or wording |
| Citation URLs | Every visible source URL, normalized for reporting |
| Sentiment and accuracy | Human-reviewed labels such as positive, neutral, negative, correct, or incorrect |

Add prompt intent, competitor names, model/version when available, and reviewer notes. Do not silently overwrite a changed answer; append a new run so the history remains meaningful. A tracker such as [AI visibility tracker](/blog/ai-visibility-tracker) can use this schema without pretending that an observation is a population-wide truth.

## How many prompts and runs are enough to start?

Thirty or more carefully selected prompts with repeated runs are a practical starting point for a small brand. Mix category questions, comparison questions, recommendation questions, problem-to-solution questions, and branded questions. Include the markets that matter commercially rather than padding the set with near-duplicates.

Repeat prompts because model answers vary with time, context, availability, and sampling. One run is a snapshot, not a stable rate. If 18 of 30 prompts mention your brand, report 60% for that observed sample and state the prompt set, engines, date range, and run count.

Sampling intervals add context, not certainty. For a proportion p observed across n independent observations, a simple Wilson interval can be calculated from:

`center = (p + z²/(2n)) / (1 + z²/n)`

`half-width = z × sqrt((p(1-p) + z²/(4n)) / n) / (1 + z²/n)`

With z = 1.96, this gives a 95% interval under a simplified model. AI runs are often correlated and are not a random sample of every possible answer, so label the result as an interval for your observed sample—not as a guarantee about all future answers. NIST provides the statistical background for Wilson intervals [here](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/prc/section2/prc241.htm).

## Which answer metrics reveal visibility and recommendation quality?

Answer metrics describe what the model actually said. Keep counts and rates visible together:

- **Mention rate:** runs with a brand mention ÷ eligible runs.
- **Recommendation rate:** runs where the brand is actively recommended ÷ eligible runs.
- **Top-choice rate:** runs where the brand is the first recommendation ÷ eligible runs.
- **Share of answer set:** brand mentions ÷ all named brands in comparable answers.
- **Accuracy rate:** accurate brand claims ÷ reviewed brand claims.
- **Sentiment mix:** positive, neutral, and negative mention counts.
- **Position distribution:** median and percentile position when an ordered list appears.

Do not hide negative observations inside an average. A 70% mention rate with frequent factual errors is a different business situation from a 50% mention rate with accurate, high-intent recommendations. Segment every metric by engine, prompt intent, geography, and date.

## Which citation metrics show source authority?

Citation metrics describe the sources an engine exposes, not the traffic those sources receive. Useful measures include citation presence rate, owned-domain citation rate, unique cited-page count, citation share by page type, and competitor citation overlap.

For example:

`owned citation rate = runs citing an owned URL ÷ runs with at least one citation`

Track whether the cited URL is current, relevant to the prompt, and factually aligned with the answer. A high citation count can be misleading if one outdated page is repeated. Report both unique URLs and citation events, then show the pages and prompts behind the totals.

Google explains that AI features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode are included in Search Console’s overall Web search reporting; Google does not provide a separate AI-feature breakout in that report. Read the official guidance [here](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features). Bing’s AI Performance public preview adds citation-oriented reporting, but citations still do not prove a conversion or even a visit; see the [Bing announcement](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2026/2/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview/).

## What can GA4 and Search Console prove about downstream impact?

GA4 can measure sessions, users, landing pages, engagement, key events, and revenue when tracking is configured correctly. Use a conservative channel definition for identifiable AI referrals, preserve UTM parameters where you control the link, and compare landing-page behavior with other acquisition sources. The [GA4 documentation](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9756891?hl=en) explains how traffic acquisition dimensions work.

GA4 cannot prove an unclicked AI influence, and “direct” traffic should not be reclassified as AI traffic without evidence. Search Console can show Google search performance, but its Web totals include AI Overview and AI Mode traffic without a clean AI-only line item. Treat clicks, impressions, and conversions as downstream measures with their own denominators.

## How should an AI search analytics dashboard be structured?

Use three panels with a shared filter bar, not one blended score:

1. **Answers:** mention, recommendation, accuracy, sentiment, and position by prompt, engine, market, and period.
2. **Citations:** citation presence, owned-domain share, page coverage, freshness, and competitor overlap.
3. **Business outcomes:** AI-referral sessions, landing pages, key events, assisted revenue where defensible, and unresolved attribution gaps.

Show the sample size beside every rate. Add a “data quality” panel listing missing runs, changed prompts, unavailable engines, and reviewer coverage. A monthly view should show the current period, prior period, and a small table of changed prompts so stakeholders can inspect why a line moved. For conversion-focused context, compare this approach with [AI traffic analytics](/blog/ai-traffic-analytics) and [AI brand recommendation measurement](/blog/ai-brand-recommendation-measurement).

## How often should teams rerun and review the data?

Run a stable core prompt set weekly or biweekly when the category changes quickly, and review trends monthly. Add an event-driven run after a major product launch, pricing change, rebrand, or high-priority content update. Preserve the old prompt wording so a trend is not manufactured by changing the question.

At each monthly review, ask:

- Did answer visibility change, or did the prompt mix change?
- Did citations move toward owned, current, relevant pages?
- Did identifiable visits or conversions change independently?
- Which inaccurate claim or missing source should content fix next?

Use [AI search citation evaluation metrics](/blog/ai-search-citation-evaluation-metrics) for deeper citation review and the [free AI visibility checker](/blog/free-ai-visibility-checker) for a lightweight starting observation. The goal is not a magical score. It is a traceable chain from answer evidence to source evidence to business evidence, with every denominator visible.
