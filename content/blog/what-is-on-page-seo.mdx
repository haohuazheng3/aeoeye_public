## What is on-page SEO?

On-page SEO is the practice of optimizing the elements that live on a single page — its content, title tag, headings, structure, and internal links — so search engines and readers can understand what the page covers and judge whether it deserves to rank. It's the one layer of SEO you fully control, because it's written into your own HTML and copy, not earned or configured elsewhere.

Every ranking system starts by reading the page itself. A search crawler, an AI answer engine, and a human skimming results all ask the same first question: does this page actually address what I'm looking for? On-page SEO is how you answer yes, in a way machines and people can both parse.

That answer has to work on two levels at once. Search engines need clean signals — a title that matches the topic, headings that organize the argument, text that covers the subject completely. Readers need something more basic: a fast, clear answer to the question that brought them there. Good on-page SEO does both jobs with the same set of choices, which is why it's the foundation the other two pillars of SEO build on.

## On-page vs off-page vs technical

SEO splits into three pillars: on-page (what's on the page itself), off-page (signals earned elsewhere, like backlinks and mentions), and technical (how well the site can be crawled, rendered, and indexed). All three have to work together — strength in one doesn't cover for failure in another.

- On-page covers content, titles, headings, and internal links — everything you write and structure directly.
- Off-page covers backlinks, brand mentions, reviews, and other external signals that vouch for the page. See [what off-page SEO is](/blog/what-is-off-page-seo) for how that layer gets built.
- Technical covers site speed, crawlability, indexing, and mobile rendering — the infrastructure that lets the other two pillars get seen at all. See [what technical SEO is](/blog/what-is-technical-seo) for the full breakdown.

The three pillars fail independently, which is what makes them worth separating. A page with excellent on-page optimization but a misconfigured robots.txt file — a technical problem — may never get indexed. A perfectly indexed, well-written page with zero external validation — an off-page gap — will struggle to rank for anything competitive. Treat all three as one system you maintain continuously, not a checklist you finish once.

## The core on-page elements

Seven elements carry most of the weight in on-page SEO: the title tag, headings, content quality, keyword and intent match, internal links, alt text, and meta description. Each does a distinct job — get these right and you've covered the fundamentals that every other on-page tactic builds on.

| Element | What it does | Best practice |
|---|---|---|
| Title tag | Tells search engines and searchers the page's core topic; it's the headline shown in results | Lead with the primary keyword, stay under about 60 characters, make it specific enough to earn the click |
| Headings (H1–H3) | Organize content into a logical hierarchy so engines and readers can scan the structure | Use one H1 per page, phrase H2s and H3s as the questions readers are actually asking |
| Content quality | Answers the query thoroughly enough that the reader doesn't need to leave and search again | Cover the topic in depth, support claims with data, keep facts current |
| Keyword/intent match | Confirms the page satisfies what the searcher actually wants, not just the words they typed | Match the dominant intent — informational, commercial, or transactional — shown in current search results |
| Internal links | Passes authority and context between related pages and helps engines map the site | Link to genuinely related pages using descriptive anchor text instead of generic phrases |
| Alt text | Describes images for screen readers, accessibility tools, and image-search crawlers | Describe what's actually in the image, in plain, specific language |
| Meta description | Doesn't directly affect rankings but shapes whether someone clicks from the results page | Write a specific, benefit-driven summary under about 155 characters |

Meta descriptions deserve extra attention because they're easy to skip and easy to get wrong. They're your pitch, not your content — see [what a meta description is](/blog/what-is-a-meta-description) for how to write ones that earn the click instead of the generic snippet search engines fall back on.

## On-page best practices

Four habits separate pages that rank from pages that don't: match search intent, structure content clearly, write genuinely useful copy, and link internally with purpose. None of these are complicated — they're just easy to skip under deadline pressure.

- Match intent. Check what's already ranking before you write. If the top 10 results are all comparison lists, a single-product review page is fighting the wrong format, no matter how well it's written.
- Structure clearly. Break content into scannable sections with descriptive subheadings. A reader — and a crawler — should be able to skim your H2s alone and know what the page covers.
- Write useful content. Answer the core question in the first few sentences, then go deeper. Cut anything that doesn't add new information; padding a page to hit a word count hurts more than it helps.
- Link internally with purpose. Connect each page to a handful of closely related pages, not every page on the site. Purposeful linking spreads authority and gives readers a path to the next answer they need.

Get these four right consistently, and the individual elements in the table above stop being a checklist and start being habits.

## On-page SEO in the AI era

On-page SEO now has a fifth job: making passages easy for AI systems to extract and quote. The classic elements — headings, structure, clarity — still matter exactly as much as before. They just serve a second audience now, alongside the human reader and the search crawler.

When ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or Google's AI Overviews answer a question, they typically don't send someone to your page first — they pull a passage, summarize it, and sometimes cite the source. That changes what well-optimized means in practice. A page can rank on page one of classic search results and still get skipped entirely by AI answer engines, if nothing on the page is written in a form that's easy to lift out.

Four additions matter most:

- Answer-first structure. Put the direct answer in the first sentence or two of each section, not after three paragraphs of setup.
- Extractable passages. Write sentences that make sense standing alone, since that's exactly how AI systems use them — pulled out of their surrounding paragraph.
- Schema markup. FAQ, Article, and HowTo schema give engines explicit, structured signals about what each block of content actually is.
- Entity clarity. Use consistent, unambiguous names for your brand, product, and key concepts so engines can connect mentions of you across different sources.

Alt text has a role here too. Descriptive, accurate [alt text](/blog/what-is-alt-text) gives multimodal AI systems another concrete signal about what a page contains, beyond the visible copy alone.

## The new on-page checklist item: quotability

Quotability means writing so a single passage — one or two sentences — can be lifted whole into an AI answer and still make complete sense on its own. It's the newest on-page factor, and most existing pages weren't built with it in mind.

Traditional on-page SEO optimized for the page as a whole: does the title match the query, does the content cover the topic, is the structure logical. Quotability adds a smaller unit of measurement underneath all of that — does this one paragraph hold up when it's the only thing someone sees?

To write quotable passages:

- Open each section with a complete, standalone answer — avoid pronouns or references that only make sense if the reader saw the paragraph above.
- Keep the core answer short enough to quote, roughly 40 to 60 words, then expand with supporting detail afterward.
- State facts plainly instead of burying them in hedged, marketing-toned language that adds words but no meaning.
- Use specific numbers and named sources where you have them; vague claims are harder for an AI system to trust and cite.

This isn't a replacement for the rest of on-page SEO — it's a layer on top of the same fundamentals. A page with clear headings, genuine answers, and sensible structure was already halfway to being quotable. It just needs to go the rest of the way, deliberately, sentence by sentence.

## How do you know if your on-page SEO is working for AI search?

The only real test is whether AI models actually cite you when someone asks a relevant question — everything else is a proxy. Rankings, traffic, and even schema validation only confirm the page is technically sound, not whether ChatGPT or Gemini chose to quote it.

On-page SEO used to be graded by one reader: a search crawler. Now it's graded by two, and the second one — the AI model deciding whether your passage is worth quoting — doesn't publish a ranking report you can check manually.
