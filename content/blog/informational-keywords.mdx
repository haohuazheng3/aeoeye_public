Search intent sorts into four types: informational, navigational, commercial, and transactional. Each one wants a different kind of page, and building the wrong one is the single most common reason a piece of content ranks, gets clicks, and still doesn't perform. This guide breaks down all four, shows you how to tag a keyword list by intent in minutes, and covers what changes now that AI engines answer the question before anyone reaches a results page.

## What are the four types of search intent keywords?

Informational keywords want an answer ("how does X work"). Navigational queries want a specific site ("Ahrefs login"). Commercial intent keywords want a comparison before a decision ("best rank tracker"). Transactional keywords want to act right now ("buy," "price," "coupon"). None of these four phrases pull huge volume on their own — "informational keywords" gets roughly 140 searches a month, "transactional keywords" and "commercial intent keywords" sit around 110 each, "navigational query" closer to 70 — but the framework behind them decides how your entire site gets organized.

| Intent type | User goal | Example patterns | Content that wins | Monetization proximity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Informational | Understand a concept or solve a problem | "what is," "how to," "why does," "guide to" | Explainers, how-tos, glossary definitions, original research | Low — builds trust and citations, rarely converts directly |
| Navigational | Reach a specific brand, tool, or page | Brand name alone, brand + login, brand + pricing | Homepage, login page, accurate branded landing pages | None directly — protects demand you already earned |
| Commercial | Compare options before choosing | "best," "vs," "review," "alternative," "top" | Comparison posts, roundups, honest review pages | Medium — pre-purchase research, close to the decision |
| Transactional | Complete an action immediately | "buy," "price," "coupon," "near me," "free trial" | Product pages, pricing pages, checkout flows | High — the closest content gets to revenue |

Four intents, four different jobs. Mixing them up is where most keyword strategies quietly fail.

## What should you build for informational keywords?

Informational keywords need content that teaches, not sells. Build explainers, how-tos, glossary entries, and original research — anything that fully answers the question before it asks the reader for anything back.

The common mistake is routing informational traffic straight into a pitch. Someone searching "what is a rank tracker" isn't ready for a pricing table; they're ready for an answer. Push any offer to the bottom of the page, or better, to a separate page the reader can choose to visit next. The payoff for informational content isn't usually a direct conversion — it's topical authority, backlinks, and increasingly, getting quoted inside an AI-generated answer.

## What should you build for a navigational query?

Navigational queries already know where they're going. The job is making sure they land exactly there — not on a competitor's comparison page, a stale review site, or a forum thread that outranks your own homepage for your own name.

The common mistake is ignoring navigational keywords because the volume looks small and "we already rank first for our own brand." Check again. Queries that pair a brand name with review or alternative are technically navigational in origin but behave like commercial keywords in the results — and third-party sites frequently rank for them. Keep your homepage, pricing page, and login flow fast and accurate, and monitor what shows up around your own brand name.

## What should you build for commercial intent keywords?

Commercial intent keywords come from a reader who's comparing, not committing yet. Build comparison posts, "best of" roundups, alternative pages, and review content that names real tradeoffs instead of pretending every option is perfect.

The common mistake is publishing informational content for what is actually a commercial query — a "what is X" explainer for a search that's really asking "which X should I pick." Same topic, different intent, different page. Commercial content is also where trust gets tested hardest: a "best of" list that just ranks the writer's own product first, with no real comparison, gets discounted by readers and increasingly by AI engines that cross-check claims across multiple sources before repeating them.

## What should you build for transactional keywords?

Transactional keywords want the shortest possible path to completing an action. Build the actual product page, pricing page, or checkout flow — don't make the searcher read an article first to find it.

The common mistake is writing a blog post for a query that should have led to a page instead. It's telling that "transactional keywords" — the phrase itself — carries essentially zero keyword difficulty: almost nobody bothers writing an article about the concept, because most transactional intent gets satisfied by the product page directly, not by content about it. Don't be the exception that makes a searcher read 1,500 words before they can buy something.

## How do you classify your keywords fast?

Read the modifier, not just the topic. A handful of signal words will sort most of a keyword list correctly on the first pass:

- **Informational** — how, what, why, guide, tips, examples
- **Commercial** — best, top, vs, review, alternative, compare
- **Transactional** — buy, price, coupon, discount, near me, free trial, sign up
- **Navigational** — a brand name alone, or a brand name plus login, pricing, or download

Modifiers are a shortcut, not a guarantee. The real answer is whatever's already ranking — a [keyword clustering tool](/blog/keyword-clustering-tool) speeds this up by grouping keywords on SERP overlap instead of just shared words, which catches intent a modifier list alone misses. A query with zero commercial-sounding words can still carry commercial intent if Google is already filling the results with comparison content.

Use the SERP as ground truth. If the top 10 is explainer content and forum threads answering the [People Also Ask](/blog/people-also-ask) box, that keyword is informational, whatever it looks like on paper. If it's product pages and comparison charts, it's commercial or transactional, and no explainer is going to outrank them. Google's own [Search Quality Rater Guidelines](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf) use a parallel taxonomy — Know, Do, Website, and Visit-in-Person queries — to train the humans who audit search results. When a page doesn't match what a rater would expect for the query type, it doesn't rank, regardless of writing quality.

## How does search intent change in the AI-answer era?

AI chat answers collapse the funnel. A single conversation can move from informational to commercial to a brand recommendation in two or three turns, without the user ever seeing a SERP, a comparison page, or a piece of content you built for that exact moment.

Ask ChatGPT "what's affiliate marketing," then follow up with "what tools help track affiliate links" — and that second question — commercial intent by any classic definition — often ends in a named brand recommendation, not a list of blue links. There's no page to rank on. The intent behind that single prompt, sometimes called [prompt intent](/glossary/prompt-intent), can shift with every follow-up the user types, inside a conversation you can't optimize a landing page for.

Your brand needs to already be the answer by the time that turn happens. That's a different problem than ranking, and it's the one [AEOeye](/) audits: whether ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Google AI, or Claude actually recommends you when someone asks the buyer-intent question, not just whether you'd rank for it.

## How do you build an intent-balanced content plan?

Treat the four intent types as a portfolio, not a checklist. Informational content builds topical authority and earns citations; commercial content converts researchers into buyers; navigational content protects the brand demand you've already earned. The right mix shifts as a site matures.

- **Early stage, low authority** — lean heavily informational. Build depth before competing on money terms; neither Google nor an AI engine trusts a new site's commercial claims yet.
- **Established, earning trust** — shift budget toward commercial pages: comparisons, roundups, alternative pages. You've earned the right to compete on "best" and "vs" queries.
- **Mature site** — commercial and transactional pages carry conversion weight, informational content keeps expanding topical ground and feeding AI citations, and navigational content stays a small but non-negotiable slice of the plan.
