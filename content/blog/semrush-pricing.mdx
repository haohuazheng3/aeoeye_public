## How much does Semrush cost?

Semrush costs a premium-tier subscription, billed monthly or annually, with multiple plans plus custom pricing for larger teams. Exact figures shift constantly — new tiers, regional tests, seasonal promos — so treat any number you see, including here, as a starting point rather than gospel. Check Semrush's own pricing page before you budget anything.

Most of what ranks for "Semrush pricing" right now is Semrush's own marketing or an affiliate page hoping you'll click through and buy. Neither has much reason to tell you where the plan boundaries actually bite. We're not selling Semrush, so we don't need you to pick the expensive tier.

What those pages won't spell out as clearly is how the pricing actually behaves once you're a customer: what counts against your limits, which features sit behind paid add-ons, and where a "starter" plan quietly turns into an "agency" bill. That's the part worth understanding before you commit a year of budget to it. This guide covers the structure — how the tiers scale, where costs hide, and a straight answer on whether it's worth paying for, by the kind of user you actually are.

## How Semrush pricing is structured

Semrush pricing scales along four main levers: projects (sites you track), keywords monitored in Position Tracking, seats (users on the account), and report or export limits. Add-on modules — including its AI visibility toolkit — are priced separately, stacked on top of whichever base plan you pick.

Here's what typically moves the needle:

- **Projects.** Each domain you actively track usually counts against a plan cap. Agencies managing a roster of client sites tend to hit this ceiling before they hit any other limit.
- **Keywords tracked.** Position Tracking caps how many keywords you can monitor per project. Go over, and you're choosing between trimming the list or upgrading a tier.
- **Seats.** Base plans ship with a small number of users. Extra seats bill per person, per month — this is where the advertised price quietly becomes a team-sized bill.
- **Reports and exports.** White-label PDF reports, API access, and export volume differ by tier. Agencies reporting to clients feel this limit fastest; solo users rarely notice it.
- **Add-on modules.** Local SEO tools, social media management, and — more recently — AI visibility tracking are sold as bolt-ons rather than bundled into the base plans. We've covered that specific module in our [Semrush AI toolkit](/blog/semrush-ai-toolkit) breakdown, since it's become one of the more common upsells.

One structural fact worth knowing before you compare plans: like nearly everything in this software category, annual billing carries a real discount over paying month to month. Semrush's own pricing page shows both side by side. If you're reasonably confident you'll stick around past month three, doing that math upfront usually beats defaulting to monthly out of caution.

## Is Semrush worth it?

Semrush is worth it if you're logging in most days — agencies running multiple client accounts, in-house teams doing weekly audits and rank tracking, content teams doing keyword research at real volume. It stops being worth it the moment your usage drops to a handful of sessions a month.

Here's a test we'd actually stand behind, because it's more honest than "it depends": list ten things you'd use Semrush for in a normal month. Competitor gap analysis, backlink audits, rank tracking, content briefs, technical site audits — whatever's real for your workflow. Get to ten without stretching, and the subscription is probably paying for itself in time saved. Pad the list to justify a login you'd nearly forgotten about, and you're paying agency-grade rates for a tool that mostly idles.

Agencies and heavy in-house teams land on "yes" without much internal debate — the tool touches prospecting, delivery, and client reporting every week, so the cost absorbs into billable work. Solo consultants, small-business owners doing their own SEO, and anyone running SEO as one job among five tend to land between "it depends" and "no." Neither answer is wrong. It comes down to whether the tool matches how often you actually work inside it.

If you're still unsure after the list test, look at your last 30 days of actual logins rather than your intentions for the next 30. Tools get budgeted based on plans; they get renewed based on habits.

## The hidden costs

The sticker price is rarely the real price. Add a second seat, bolt on the AI visibility module, or blow past your keyword-tracking limit mid-quarter, and your effective monthly cost climbs well past the plan you originally picked.

The usual culprits:

- **Seat math.** Per-seat billing means a three-person team doesn't pay one flat rate — it pays a base plan plus per-seat add-ons, and that math rarely gets modeled correctly at signup.
- **Add-on creep.** Each specialized module — AI visibility, local listings, social — is its own line item. Teams that start with one plan often carry two or three add-ons within a year, without ever consciously deciding to.
- **Limit overages.** Exceeding your project or keyword cap forces an upgrade decision mid-cycle, usually at a worse per-unit rate than if you'd picked the higher tier from day one.
- **Ramp-up time.** Not a line on the invoice, but real. Semrush is deep enough that teams routinely underestimate how long it takes before the tool pays itself back in actual output.
- **Currency and regional pricing.** Some pricing pages show different effective rates depending on region or billing currency; always check the number in your own currency rather than a headline figure from a review written elsewhere.

## Cheaper paths that actually work

You don't need Semrush's full stack to do good SEO. Value-tier all-in-one platforms cover most of the day-to-day workflow at a fraction of the price, point tools handle single jobs — rank tracking, backlink checks, keyword research — without the bundle markup, and Google's own free tools cover real ground for zero dollars.

We've mapped the specific swaps in [our Semrush alternatives guide](/blog/semrush-alternatives) — which tool replaces which feature, and where the real gaps show up once you leave Semrush's ecosystem. The short version: most solo operators and small teams can run a leaner, cheaper stack and only feel the difference on the handful of things that are genuinely hard to replace, like the size of Semrush's backlink index. For everyone else, the premium price is mostly buying convenience and depth you may not be using. The real question isn't which tool is cheapest on paper — it's which jobs you actually need done every week, and which ones you're paying to keep "just in case."

## The visibility Semrush doesn't price

Semrush prices and tracks rank in traditional search results — the ten blue links. It doesn't natively price or measure whether ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, or Google's AI Overviews mention your brand when someone asks a buying question. That's a separate visibility problem, and search behavior is shifting toward it fast enough that a rank-tracking-only view is already incomplete for a lot of buyer journeys.

That gap is exactly why AI-visibility add-ons and dedicated platforms have started showing up around tools like Semrush — worth understanding on its own terms rather than assumed to be covered by a rank-tracking subscription.
