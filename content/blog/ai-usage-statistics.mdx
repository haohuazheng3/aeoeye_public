## AI usage statistics: where things stand (2026)

As of 2025, about 34% of US adults have used ChatGPT at least once — nearly double the share who had tried it in 2023, according to Pew Research Center. That's one of the fastest adoption curves any consumer technology has posted in the US.

The platform-level numbers back it up. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said ChatGPT had reached roughly 800 million weekly active users as of October 2025, a figure reported by TechCrunch. That's not a niche tool anymore — it's a mainstream habit, on the scale of a top global app.

The story isn't limited to one company, either. Google's Gemini app reported roughly 750 million monthly active users as of February 2026, also per TechCrunch. Two of the largest consumer AI products in the world are now counting active users in the hundreds of millions, at the same time.

Here's the thing worth sitting with: these numbers move fast, and most of them come from the companies themselves, not independent audits. We'll get to why that matters. First, the headline figures worth knowing.

## Consumer AI adoption at a glance

Skip the noise. These four numbers are the ones we'd actually stand behind, because each one traces back to a named, checkable source.

| Metric | Figure | Source | As of |
|---|---|---|---|
| US adults who have used ChatGPT | ~34% | Pew Research Center | 2025 |
| ChatGPT weekly active users | ~800 million | TechCrunch | Oct 2025 |
| Google Gemini app monthly active users | ~750 million | TechCrunch | Feb 2026 |
| ChatGPT prompts sent per day | ~2.5 billion | TechCrunch | Jul 2025 |

A few things jump out. First, ChatGPT's US adoption rate (34%) is a survey number — people self-reporting whether they've ever used the tool. The 800 million weekly active users figure is a platform metric — a measured count of people who showed up in a given week. Those aren't the same kind of number, and conflating them is where a lot of AI usage statistics articles go wrong.

Second, Gemini's 750 million is a monthly figure, not weekly — Google hasn't published a directly comparable weekly number that we've found. Comparing ChatGPT's WAU to Gemini's MAU as if they're apples to apples will overstate or understate the gap depending on which way you round.

We'll unpack why that distinction matters more in the next section.

## AI in search: the shift that matters here

The number that matters most isn't how many people have an AI app installed. It's how many of them are now asking that app the kind of question they used to type into Google.

At roughly 2.5 billion prompts sent per day on ChatGPT alone as of July 2025, per TechCrunch, that's a genuinely large volume of questions, comparisons, and buying decisions happening inside a chat window instead of a search results page.

We can't put a precise number on how much search volume that's displacing — nobody has published a clean, sourced figure for that yet, and we're not going to invent one. But directionally, the pattern shows up everywhere: queries like 'best CRM for a 10-person team,' 'is this brand legit,' or 'alternatives to this tool' — the exact bottom-of-funnel questions that used to live entirely in Google's search results — are increasingly starting inside ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, or Google's AI Overviews instead.

That shift is exactly why AI visibility has become its own discipline. If a buyer asks an AI assistant to recommend a tool in your category and your brand doesn't come up, ranking #1 on Google for the same query doesn't help you — the buyer never saw a traditional results page at all. ## Why AI usage stats are messy

Three things make this category harder to report cleanly than it looks.

- **WAU vs. MAU aren't interchangeable.** Weekly active users and monthly active users measure different windows of behavior. Two products can report the same headline number and still mean very different things — one counting everyone active in a single week, the other counting everyone active across a full month, which is a much lower bar to clear.
- **Self-reported and measured data aren't the same thing.** Pew's 34% comes from asking people directly, which is subject to memory and honesty. Company-reported user counts come from server logs — more precise, but also unaudited by anyone outside the company.
- **The numbers are stale within months.** A stat that was accurate in early 2025 can be badly outdated by the time you read it in 2026. Adoption in this category hasn't leveled off yet, and it may not for a while.

None of that makes the numbers useless — it means you should check who's reporting a figure, how they measured it, and when, before you cite it. ## What rising AI usage means for your business

Here's the pivot that actually matters if you run a website, a SaaS product, or a brand: a fast-growing share of your future customers are now doing product research inside an AI assistant before they ever visit your site.

Pew's 34% and OpenAI's 800 million weekly users aren't abstract — they represent real people who, at some point most weeks, are asking an AI model questions instead of, or before, opening a search engine. Some of those questions are exactly the ones your prospects ask right before they buy: what's the best option, is a given brand worth it, what the alternatives are.

If your brand isn't part of the answer, you're invisible to that share of demand, no matter how well you rank in traditional search. This mirrors what's happening on the business side too, where companies are adopting generative AI tools internally at a comparable pace; our [generative AI statistics](/blog/generative-ai-statistics) roundup covers that side of the adoption curve in more detail.

The practical takeaway: rising AI usage isn't a trend to watch from the sidelines. It's a new discovery channel that either includes your brand or doesn't, and that outcome isn't automatic.

## Where to find trustworthy AI usage data

Stick to sources that name their methodology and publish an as-of date. In practice, that narrows down to three types.

1. **Independent research organizations.** Pew Research Center is the clearest example for US consumer adoption; it publishes its survey methodology alongside its findings, so you can judge the numbers yourself.
2. **Company disclosures.** OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic occasionally share user numbers through blog posts, earnings calls, or executive statements. These are real figures, but they're self-reported and not third-party audited.
3. **Reputable press.** Outlets like TechCrunch report, verify, and add context to company disclosures, and often catch the inconsistencies that a company's own announcement glosses over.

For a wider set of sourced figures beyond the consumer-adoption numbers covered here — model-level usage, context windows, and platform comparisons — see our [LLM statistics](/blog/llm-statistics) page. And whatever you cite, check the publish date before you use it. A quick test: if a headline statistic doesn't link back to a named study, report, or company statement, treat it as marketing copy, not data.
