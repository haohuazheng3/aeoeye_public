## What is Windsurf AI pricing now?

The current plan structure combines monthly access, model availability, and usage allowances. Free is a real lightweight tier; Pro is the practical individual starting point; Max is a specialist purchase; Team adds an organizational base and seats.

| Plan | Monthly price | Included experience | Best fit |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Free | $0 | Light agent quota, limited models, unlimited inline edits and Tab | Evaluation and small edits |
| Pro | $20 | Increased quotas and frontier models | Regular individual agent use |
| Max | $200 | Significantly higher individual quotas | Heavy, measured production use |
| Team | $80 + $40/full developer | Team access and seat allowances | Multiple governed developers |

Free can be useful for completion and lightweight changes, but its agent quota and model selection are limited. It is not a reliable substitute for sustained production work.

Pro’s value depends on whether increased quota and frontier-model access reduce interrupted or abandoned tasks. Max is justified only when substantial work consumes the allowance productively.

## How do Windsurf quotas work?

Paid plans use daily and weekly allowances, not a promise of unlimited generation. A monthly subscription does not make every request free until the next billing date.

A long debugging session, large repository context, or multi-step implementation consumes more allowance than a short edit. Additional usage can be billed at API pricing, creating this stack:

1. Pay the fixed plan price.
2. Consume daily and weekly allowances.
3. Purchase eligible additional usage at API pricing after those allowances.

Short, well-scoped tasks may fit the included capacity. Broad repository exploration and repeated implementation attempts require more monitoring. Record both allowance use and overage exposure.

## What is the cost per completed task?

Effective cost is subscription plus overage and the value of recovery time, divided by completed tasks. Measure outcomes rather than requests.

**Effective cost per completed task = (plan cost + overage cost + recovery-time value) ÷ completed tasks**

A $20 Pro plan is excellent if it helps complete twenty meaningful tasks. It is poor if quota interruptions leave two finished tasks and many partial changes.

Track whether the agent understood relevant files, produced a reviewable change, passed checks, needed manual correction, and finished before a quota reset. One request may be a rename; another may involve exploration, tests, dependencies, and a production patch.

My view is straightforward: buy capacity for completed work, not impressive model labels. Frontier models matter when they improve completion, but a name alone does not establish return.

## What does Windsurf cost for a team?

Team starts at $80 monthly plus $40 for each full developer seat. Four full seats therefore cost $80 + (4 × $40) = $240 monthly before overages.

That arithmetic is a baseline, not a total-spend promise. Teams still face daily and weekly allowances and potential API-priced usage.

Seat utilization matters. An occasional user may not justify $40, while an active developer may benefit from consistent access. Track completed tasks per seat during the first billing period.

Governance matters too. Define who can authorize overage, what limits apply, and what happens when capacity is nearly exhausted. A team plan without spending rules merely moves uncertainty from individual cards to one shared bill.

## How can you control overages?

Treat allowances as a visible budget. Check remaining daily and weekly capacity before starting large work, especially in unfamiliar repositories.

Use safeguards:

- Break work into explicit milestones.
- Ask for a plan before a broad implementation.
- Keep context focused on relevant files.
- Review changes before launching another iteration.
- Require approval for API-priced overage.
- Record unusually expensive task shapes.

“Fix the bug” invites open-ended exploration. “Reproduce the failing test, find the smallest cause, patch it, and run the targeted test” creates a bounded objective.

Refusing every overage is not automatically cheaper: a controlled overage that finishes a high-value incident may be rational. The mistake is overage with no defined value or ceiling.

## How should you run a seven-day pilot?

A seven-day pilot should test completed repository work, not whether the editor feels impressive. Use one bug, small feature, tested refactor, inline-edit session, and large-context task.

For each, record the prompt, time to first useful change, iterations, quota behavior, checks passed, and manual cleanup. Note whether failure came from reasoning, code, or an allowance boundary.

At the end, calculate completed tasks per dollar and identify where quota friction appears. That evidence is more useful than a generic speed claim.

## What is the verdict?

Free is worth trying for light agent work and unlimited inline edits and Tab. Pro at $20 is the sensible regular-use default. Max at $200 requires heavy, productive usage. Team can work when seats are active and overage is governed.

The cost trap is judging Windsurf by an old $15 screenshot or monthly price alone. Judge it by completed repository work, recovery time, and controlled total usage; use [AEOeye pricing](/pricing) separately when evaluating search-visibility work rather than code production.
