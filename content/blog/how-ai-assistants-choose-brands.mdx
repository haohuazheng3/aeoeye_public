An AI assistant names brands by one of two routes: it recalls them from training data, or it runs a live web search and reads the results. Either way it builds a small pool of candidates, drops names that independent sources don't back up, and orders what is left. No engine publishes its ranking weights, so this page sticks to what documentation and research support, and marks our own reading as ours.

## How do AI assistants decide which brands to recommend?

They work in four steps: choose a route, gather candidate names, test each name against sources, then order and describe the survivors. The first two steps decide whether you are considered at all. The last two decide how you come across.

1. **Route.** The assistant decides whether to answer from memory or to search. Anthropic's documentation says [Claude searches when a request depends on current or changing information](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/web-search-tool), including organizations and products that might have changed, and answers stable-knowledge questions directly.
2. **Candidate pool.** Names come from associations learned in training, from pages retrieved for this question, or from both.
3. **Corroboration.** Names that several independent sources support survive. A name mentioned once, or only on its own site, is easy to drop.
4. **Ordering and framing.** The survivors get ranked and labelled ("best for small teams", "cheaper alternative"), and the label comes from how sources describe you.

Buyer questions like "best CRM for a five-person agency" sit squarely in the "products that might have changed" group. In our reading, search is often part of the answer, with memory as the fallback.

## What is the difference between memory and live search?

Memory is what the model absorbed during training; live search fetches current pages and feeds them to the model as it writes. Memory is frozen at the training cutoff. Search reflects the web today, which makes it the layer where a brand can change its odds this quarter.

The research name for the second pattern is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): [a model's built-in "parametric" memory combined with an external index it can look things up in](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401). Google says its AI Overviews and AI Mode use RAG, also called grounding, to [retrieve relevant, up-to-date pages from its Search index](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) before generating a response.

| | Memory (training data) | Live search (retrieval) |
|---|---|---|
| Where names come from | Associations learned in training | Pages retrieved for this question |
| Freshness | Frozen at the training cutoff | Current index |
| How you influence it | Slowly: broad, consistent presence that a future model may absorb | Directly: appear on pages that get retrieved |
| Crawler involved | Training crawlers such as GPTBot | Search crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot |

OpenAI keeps the two separate: [GPTBot crawls content that may be used in training, while OAI-SearchBot surfaces sites in ChatGPT search](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots), and each can be allowed or disallowed independently. Our position: spend your effort on the search path. You cannot edit a model that has already been trained.

## How does a shortlist form?

A shortlist forms when the assistant collects every name it can tie to the question, removes names it cannot back up, and keeps a handful. In AEOeye's audit records, answers named about three competitors each, and a brand that was named and ranked was usually named first.

The pool is wider than your headline question. Google describes [query fan-out](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) as a set of concurrent, related queries the model generates to fetch more results: its example turns "how to fix a lawn that's full of weeds" into searches for herbicides and chemical-free removal. Our reading: you need to show up for the sub-questions behind a buying decision (price, fit, alternatives), not only for the main phrase.

In AEOeye's recommendation benchmark (127 completed audits created 26 June to 7 September 2026, 892 engine answers; [aggregates published here](/resources/aeoeye-recommendation-benchmark-2026-09.json)):

- The audited brand was named in 157 answers (17.6%).
- Answers named 2.9 competitors on average.
- Of 147 ranked mentions, 71.4% were first place (average position 1.54).
- 144 of the 157 mentions were positive and 1 negative: invisibility, not bad press, is the common problem.

The caveat is mandatory. This is a small, self-selected sample (27 brand names, repeat audits included). Engine samples are unequal and not comparable, and four of the five engines have fewer than 10 mentions. So we make no claim about which engine recommends more, and you should test your own questions.

Answers are winner-take-most, so being named at all is the hard part. Lists also move. [SparkToro's study of 2,961 runs of 12 prompts](https://sparktoro.com/blog/new-research-ais-are-highly-inconsistent-when-recommending-brands-or-products-marketers-should-take-care-when-tracking-ai-visibility/) across ChatGPT, Claude and Google's AI features and found under a 1 in 100 chance of the same brand list twice, and about 1 in 1,000 for the same list in the same order. A shortlist is a probability, so aim to be in the pool often rather than to win one run.

## Which signals matter most?

Independent corroboration matters most for any brand that is not the category leader. Research comparing web-enabled ChatGPT with Google found AI answers drawing mostly on third-party sources, and a few other signals either gate or break ties. These are our readings of that evidence, not published ranking factors.

- **Third-party corroboration.** In [Chen et al.'s comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919) (data collected August 2025), software queries in Canada drew 74.2% of AI search sources from earned media and 25.8% from brand-owned sites. Google's mix for the same queries was 53.8% brand and 31.8% earned. The authors call the absolute percentages illustrative.
- **Category leadership.** The same paper ran fifty unbranded "most popular" and "best" cola prompts: major brands made up 56.3% of ChatGPT's brand mentions (274 of 487) and 67.9% of Perplexity's (339 of 499). Popularity-framed prompts probably exaggerate the effect, yet the paper concludes that unbranded queries default to market leaders.
- **Specificity.** For niche brands the authors recommend dominating a narrow niche with deep expert content and targeted earned media. We agree: "project management for five-person design agencies" is a much smaller pool to win than "project management software".
- **Freshness.** Google says retrieval improves the freshness of responses, and Chen et al. found engines differ significantly in freshness. We have no data on how heavily any engine weights recency, so don't build a plan on it.
- **Access.** This is a gate, not a lever. OpenAI says sites opted out of OAI-SearchBot [are not shown in ChatGPT search answers](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots), so a blocked crawler makes the rest irrelevant. Check it first, though it is rarely the real cause.

## What should you do about it?

Check what the assistants say now, find the pages they pull from, then earn a place on those pages and state one narrow claim the same way everywhere. Don't write content "for AI": Google's own guide says [SEO best practices continue to apply to its generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide).

1. **Ask your buyer questions in each assistant, several times.** Log which names appear, in what order, and which sources are cited. Our [free audit](/) does a first pass on ChatGPT; repeat the questions by hand elsewhere.
2. **Separate memory from search.** If the answer cites sources, search ran, and those cited domains are your real candidate pool. If it cites nothing, you are looking at memory.
3. **Rule out the gate.** Confirm robots.txt allows OAI-SearchBot and that your CDN or firewall isn't challenging search bots.
4. **Get onto the cited pages.** Reviews, roundups and comparisons that already get retrieved are usually a better target than another post on your own domain. [Brand mentions](/blog/brand-mentions) explains how to earn them.
5. **Own one narrow claim.** Use the same name, category and "who it's for" wording on your site, profiles and third-party listings, so sources agree.
6. **Re-run the same questions after changes.** Judge by how often you appear across runs, not by one answer.

## What don't we know?

No engine publishes how it weights memory, retrieval, recency or source type, and the studies we lean on are snapshots. Treat each signal above as a hypothesis you can test on your own questions, not a guaranteed factor.

- **Weights are unpublished.** Google names the components, not how heavily each counts.
- **The research ages.** Chen et al. collected data in August 2025 and say their findings should not be treated as permanent truths, because engines keep changing.
- **Our benchmark is small.** It shows the shape of answers, not a market-wide rate.
- **Answers vary run to run.** One output can't tell you whether memory or search produced it; only cited sources hint at that.

What we do stand behind: you cannot be recommended from a pool you are not in, and the pool is built by other people's pages. Start there.
