Search for google knowledge panel and you'll mostly find two kinds of pages: Google's own help docs, which explain what a panel is but stay quiet on how to earn one, and SEO roundups that treat it like a checklist you can clear in an afternoon. Almost none of them mention the part that actually matters — a knowledge panel is just the visible proof that Google trusts your entity, and that same trust is increasingly what decides whether AI engines mention your brand at all. Keep that connection in mind, because it's the thread running through everything below.

## What is a Google Knowledge Panel?

A Google Knowledge Panel is the info box — typically on the right side of desktop search results, or stacked at the top on mobile — that appears when Google has high confidence about a specific entity: a person, brand, organization, or place. It's generated from the [Knowledge Graph](/glossary/knowledge-graph), Google's database of entities and the verified relationships between them, not scraped live from your homepage copy. It also tends to appear specifically on navigational, branded searches — someone typing your name, not a generic category term — which is itself a clue about what Google thinks it knows about you.

A panel usually includes a logo or photo, a short description, key facts such as founding date, headquarters, or founders, links to verified social profiles, and a carousel of related entities people also search for. None of that is something you type into a form — it's assembled from what Google's systems already believe about you.

The confusion with Business Profiles is constant enough to kill off in one paragraph. A Business Profile (the listing that used to be called Google My Business) is a local listing tied to a physical address or service area — you claim it directly, control the hours, photos, and posts, and it shows up with a map pin and reviews. A knowledge panel is an entity summary from the Knowledge Graph; it can exist for a software product, a national brand with no storefront, or a public figure, and you can't claim one into existence — only after it appears. A local business can have both at once: a Business Profile for the map listing and a knowledge panel for the entity behind it.

## How do you get a knowledge panel?

You don't apply for a knowledge panel — there's no submission form for most entities — you earn one by building enough entity confidence that Google's systems decide you're worth summarizing on their own. That confidence gets built the same way for a two-person startup as it does for a household name, just at very different speeds, and newer entities should expect this to take sustained, ordinary work rather than a single fix.

The moves that actually move the needle:

- Keep entity info consistent everywhere. The same name, description, and core facts on your website, your social profiles, and any directories you're listed in. Contradictions stall confidence far more than gaps do.
- Add Organization schema with sameAs. Structured markup tells Google directly what it would otherwise have to infer, and the sameAs property explicitly links your official social and reference profiles back to your site. This is the same [structured data that helps AI engines parse and trust your site](/blog/structured-data-for-ai) — it's doing double duty.
- Show up in the sources the Knowledge Graph actually draws from. Wikipedia and Wikidata are two of them, where you genuinely merit an entry — but be honest with yourself here: Wikipedia has notability standards and conflict-of-interest rules, and a self-promotional page tends to get flagged or deleted, which can hurt you more than having no page at all. Don't treat it as a growth hack.
- Earn press coverage you didn't write yourself. Independent publications mentioning and describing you is corroboration Google can't get from your own site, no matter how well-optimized it is.
- Claim and verify the panel once it appears. Google documents a verification process for the entity a panel represents, and a way to suggest edits to the facts shown. Both are real tools — but they only work after a panel exists; they're not how you conjure one into existence.

All of this sits under what's usually called [entity SEO](/glossary/entity-seo): optimizing for how machines understand what you are, rather than which keywords your pages happen to rank for.

| Entity signal | What it tells Google | Where to fix it |
|---|---|---|
| Consistent name & description everywhere | You're one coherent entity, not several fragmented ones | Site About page, social bios, directory listings |
| Organization schema with sameAs | Explicitly connects your official profiles to your website | Site template / CMS structured data settings |
| Independent press mentions | Corroborates facts about you from sources you don't control | Outreach, contributed articles, trade coverage |
| Wikipedia / Wikidata presence (where merited) | Feeds the Knowledge Graph with structured, sourced facts | Only pursue if you genuinely meet notability guidelines |
| Citations on reputable directories | Reinforces identity and legitimacy from multiple angles | Industry-specific and general business directories |

## Why knowledge panels matter more in the AI era

A knowledge panel isn't the prize — it's a symptom. It shows up once Google's entity confidence about you crosses a threshold, and that same underlying graph data is a meaningful part of what feeds Google AI Overviews and grounds what other AI assistants say about your brand. Nobody selling knowledge panel checklists tends to make that connection explicit, which is a gap worth closing.

Think about what a large language model actually has to work with when someone asks it to recommend a brand in your category. It isn't calling your sales team. It's drawing on the same kind of aggregated, corroborated entity data that produces a knowledge panel: consistent facts, independent validation, and clear relationships between you and the category you operate in. When that data is thin, ambiguous, or contradictory, you don't get a panel — and for the same reasons, you often don't get mentioned either. Those are the same failure, not two separate problems, which is exactly what most advice on [why brands go invisible to AI](/blog/why-your-brand-is-invisible-to-ai) misses when it treats the two as unrelated.

That's also why we'd push back on treating the panel itself as the goal. Chase entity confidence, and a panel is one of several things that can follow. Chase the panel directly, and you'll spend effort on cosmetic fixes that don't move the underlying signal at all. If you want to know where you actually stand, the more useful question isn't whether you have a panel — it's [what AI engines are currently saying about your brand when someone asks](/answers/how-to-check-if-ai-mentions-your-brand), which you can see directly instead of inferring it from a SERP feature. ## Common reasons you don't have one

Most brands without a knowledge panel aren't missing some secret trick — they're stuck on one of four recurring problems, and each is diagnosable if you're honest about which one applies to you.

- Ambiguous naming. You share a name with a bigger, older, or simply more established entity — a band, a public figure, another company — and Google's systems can't confidently disambiguate who you are from the search demand around that name.
- Thin third-party corroboration. Your own site clearly says who you are, but almost nothing independent confirms it: no press, no reference-source mentions, nothing outside your own control.
- Inconsistent facts across the web. Different descriptions, founding dates, or even name variants across your site, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and old directory listings. Contradictions actively suppress confidence; they don't just fail to help it.
- No structured data. Without Organization schema, Google has to infer your identity from unstructured text instead of being told directly, which slows the whole process down for no good reason.

None of these are permanent, and none require a rebrand or an agency retainer to fix. They're just ordinary, unglamorous work — and it happens to be the same work that determines whether AI engines trust you enough to bring your brand up unprompted.
