## What is a backlink?

A backlink is a link from one website to another. When Site A links to Site B, Site B has earned a backlink — and search engines have treated that link as a vote of confidence for more than two decades. It's one of the oldest, most durable ranking signals in SEO.

Google's PageRank algorithm, introduced in 1998, was built on exactly this idea: a link is an endorsement, and pages with more (and better) endorsements rank higher. Modern ranking systems are far more sophisticated than the original PageRank formula, but the underlying logic never left. Other sites vouching for yours still counts.

You'll also see backlinks called "inbound links" or "incoming links." All three describe the same thing: someone else, on their own site, chose to point at yours.

One thing to get straight before going further: not every backlink is worth the same. A single link from a trusted, relevant, well-trafficked site can outweigh dozens of links from low-quality directories. What separates the two is covered in detail below.

## Why backlinks matter for SEO

Backlinks matter because they're one of the clearest trust signals a search engine can measure from outside your own site. Anyone can claim their content is excellent. A backlink is someone else confirming it, in public, with their own reputation attached.

Three concrete things happen when a page earns strong backlinks:

- **Authority transfers.** Trust and ranking power flow from the linking site to the page it links to — what SEOs call "link equity."
- **Discovery speeds up.** Crawlers follow links to find new content. A backlink from a frequently crawled site can get a new page indexed faster than waiting on a sitemap alone.
- **Referral traffic follows.** People click links. A backlink on a high-traffic page sends real visitors, independent of any ranking impact.

Here's where most site owners get it wrong: they chase volume instead of quality. A hundred backlinks from link farms and comment spam won't outrank ten backlinks from sites your industry actually respects — and a pile of spammy links can trigger a manual action or algorithmic devaluation that costs you far more than it ever earned. Quality over quantity isn't a slogan here; it's the entire game. The same principle governs [domain authority](/blog/what-is-domain-authority) as a whole — it's built from the quality of what links to you, not the count.

## Types of backlinks

Not every backlink is earned, structured, or valued the same way. Here's how the main categories compare.

| Backlink type | Value | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Editorial / earned | High — the site linked because the content deserved it | Low, as long as the content genuinely merits the link |
| Guest post | Medium — solid when the site and topic are a real fit | Medium — mass guest-posting networks get penalized |
| Directory / listing | Low to medium — useful for niche or local directories | Low, but generic directories add little and can look spammy |
| Nofollow | Low direct ranking value — signals "don't pass authority" | Low — safe, and still useful for traffic and exposure |
| Dofollow | High — passes link equity by default | Depends entirely on the quality of the linking source |

Nofollow versus dofollow trips up almost everyone starting out. `rel="nofollow"` is an HTML attribute telling search engines not to pass ranking credit through that link — you'll see it on comments, forum posts, and most press releases. Dofollow is simply the absence of that attribute; it's the default. Google has said for years it treats nofollow as a "hint" rather than an absolute rule, meaning even nofollow links can carry some weight in aggregate, especially at scale from reputable domains.

The anchor text attached to any of these links matters too — it's a separate signal search engines use to understand what the linked page is actually about. That deserves its own explanation; see our guide to [anchor text](/blog/what-is-anchor-text) for how to handle it well.

## What makes a good backlink

A good backlink is relevant, from an authoritative source, earned editorially, and placed in real context — never bought, swapped, or stuffed into a footer. Four traits separate a link that helps from one that does nothing:

- **Relevant.** The linking site operates in or near your topic. A woodworking blog linking to a chisel-sharpening guide makes sense; an unrelated insurance site does not.
- **Authoritative.** The linking domain has earned its own trust — real traffic, a real content history, a real reputation. You can't borrow authority from a site that has none.
- **Editorial.** Someone linked because your content genuinely helped their reader, not because you paid for the placement or traded links in a private network.
- **Contextual.** The link sits inside real body content about your topic, not bolted onto a sidebar or a "resources" page that exists purely to hold outbound links.

Paid link schemes, private blog networks, and reciprocal link exchanges all violate Google's spam policies. They can work briefly. They rarely last, and when spam systems catch up, the penalty usually costs more than the scheme ever earned. This whole discipline — building your site's reputation from outside your own pages — falls under [off-page SEO](/blog/what-is-off-page-seo).

## Backlinks in the AI search era

Backlinks still matter in AI search, but their job has shifted: AI engines use links and mentions as corroboration, not just as crawl paths. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI Overviews, and Claude don't return ten blue links — they synthesize an answer and decide, source by source, who earns a citation.

That decision leans on the same underlying signal backlinks have always provided: trust. When multiple independent, credible sites link to (or discuss) the same source on a topic, that's corroboration. Models trained on that pattern learn to weight corroborated information more heavily, and retrieval systems built on top of them favor sources with strong, consistent link and mention profiles. A page nothing else on the web references gives an AI model no external reason to trust it.

Here's what's genuinely different now: unlinked brand mentions carry real weight alongside traditional backlinks. If credible publications mention your brand by name in your industry's context — even without a clickable link — that still functions as a corroborating signal in the entity graph these models build. Google has described a version of this for years as "implied links," and generative AI search has raised the stakes on it. Your entity profile — who this brand is, and whether it can be trusted — gets built from links, mentions, reviews, and citations across the web, not from anchor text alone.

## How to earn backlinks (the durable way)

The durable way to earn backlinks is to build something worth linking to, then make sure the right people know it exists. That's the whole strategy, minus the shortcuts. Two moves carry most of the weight:

- **Build linkable assets.** Original research, genuinely useful tools, comprehensive guides, and clear data all get linked to because they solve a problem better than the alternatives. Nobody links to thin content out of charity.
- **Run real digital PR.** Pitch journalists and industry writers original data, expert commentary, or a genuinely useful resource — not a generic guest-post request. Good coverage is coverage that would have happened even if you'd never asked for a link back.

Everything else — outreach templates, broken-link building, unlinked-mention reclamation — is tactics layered on that foundation, and none of it works if the underlying content doesn't deserve the link.
