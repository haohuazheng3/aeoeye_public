For most of the last decade, browser market share was one of the most boring metrics in tech — Chrome dominant, everyone else fighting over scraps, nothing to see here. That changed the moment AI companies decided the browser itself was worth owning.

Perplexity shipped Comet. OpenAI shipped Atlas. Google started folding Gemini into Chrome like it was defending territory, because it is. Here's what the numbers actually show, and why a market that looked settled is suddenly a battleground again.

## What is the browser market share right now?

Chrome holds a commanding lead — roughly two-thirds of the global browser market — with Safari a clear, distant second thanks almost entirely to the iPhone. Edge and Firefox trail well behind that, and everyone else, including the new AI browsers, is currently a rounding error.

That hierarchy has been stable for years, but the exact numbers shift every month, and a blog post is the wrong place to freeze them in time. StatCounter tracks this in near real time from billions of page views, and its [live browser market share tracker](https://gs.statcounter.com/browser-market-share) is genuinely the better source if you need the current split down to a decimal point. We'd rather point you to accurate numbers than publish stale ones dressed up as current.

What doesn't change month to month is the shape of the market: one dominant player, one strong mobile-driven challenger, and a long tail fighting for single-digit scraps. That shape is exactly what the AI browser makers are betting they can finally break.

## Why Chrome dominates

Chrome's lead isn't an accident of quality — it's an accident of distribution, reinforced by a decade of lock-in that makes switching feel like a downgrade. Four things do most of the work:

- **Default placement at scale.** Chrome ships as the default on most Android devices worldwide, putting it in front of billions of people who never open a settings menu to change it.
- **Cross-device sync.** Bookmarks, saved passwords, open tabs, and extensions follow you from phone to laptop to desktop. Switching browsers means rebuilding that continuity from scratch, and most people won't bother.
- **Chromium as the web's baseline.** Chrome's engine, Chromium, also powers Edge, Brave, Opera, and others, which means the modern web gets built and tested against it first. Compatibility problems on Chrome are rare almost by design.
- **The Google account loop.** Anyone signed into Search, Gmail, Docs, or YouTube gets nudged toward Chrome for a more seamless experience, and Chrome quietly reinforces Google Search as the default search engine in return. Each product sells the other.

None of these require Chrome to be better than the alternatives on any given day — only stickier.

## The mobile vs desktop split

Combine desktop and mobile into one number and you miss the real story: browser share splits hard along device lines, and the reasons are almost entirely about defaults, not preference.

Safari's strength is almost a pure function of the iPhone. Apple's tightly controlled default setup keeps Safari a clear second worldwide, even though it barely registers as a desktop choice outside the Mac.

Edge is the mirror image: it's the default on Windows, enough to hold third place, but it has never broken through on mobile or macOS. Firefox and the smaller players are, at this point, almost entirely opt-in choices — a slower-growing pool than 'came pre-installed and nobody changed it.'

That's the pattern to hold onto: browser share mostly measures who controls the default, not who wins a fair fight.

## The browser landscape, player by player

Every browser in this market is playing a different game — some are defending a default, some are chasing AI relevance, some are betting on privacy. Here's how the major players line up strategically.

| Browser | Backer | Position Posture | Strategic Angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chrome | Google | Dominant, defending | Fold Gemini into the toolbar rather than risk losing the default |
| Safari | Apple | Strong on mobile, quiet on desktop | Own the iPhone default; lead with privacy as the differentiator |
| Edge | Microsoft | Steady, Windows-only strength | Bundle Copilot in; lean on OS-level default placement |
| Firefox | Mozilla (non-profit) | Small, stable, opt-in | Independence and privacy for people who deliberately left Chrome |
| Comet | Perplexity | New entrant, AI-first | Replace search-and-click with ask-and-answer inside the browser itself |
| Atlas | OpenAI | New entrant, AI-first | Make ChatGPT the default interface for acting on the web |
| Brave | Brave Software | Niche, privacy/crypto-leaning | Block ads and trackers by default; court the anti-Google audience |

## Why browser share suddenly matters again: the AI browser wars

Browser share went quiet for years because the winner was obvious and the product barely changed. That truce ended when AI companies decided the browser — not the search box — is becoming the place where questions get answered, and started building their own to prove it.

For most of the 2010s and into the early 2020s, the browser was a solved problem. You picked one, it synced your stuff, and you never thought about it again. Share moved by fractions of a point per quarter, and the biggest news was Microsoft occasionally relaunching Edge.

That stability broke once generative AI moved from 'chatbot in a tab' to 'thing that wants to run the whole session.'

Perplexity's [Comet](/blog/comet-browser) turns the browser into an agent that reads pages, compares tabs, and answers questions without you leaving the address bar. OpenAI's [Atlas](/blog/chatgpt-atlas) makes ChatGPT the default way to navigate and act on the web, not just a site you occasionally visit.

Neither company is chasing browser share for its own sake — they're chasing the moment a question gets asked, and the browser is the least contested real estate left to do that in.

Google noticed. Gemini's expanding footprint inside Chrome — summarizing pages, drafting replies, answering questions inline — is less a new feature than a defensive perimeter.

If a meaningful slice of users start asking questions inside Comet or Atlas instead of typing them into a Google search bar, Chrome's install-base dominance stops mattering nearly as much as who actually answers the question.

Both newcomers are still early, and neither has meaningfully dented Chrome's numbers yet. That's not the same thing as saying they won't.

## The search connection

Whoever controls the default browser effectively controls the default search engine, and that pairing is exactly why browser share is worth tracking in the first place. Chrome's dominance is a major reason Google still commands 90.46% of global search share versus Bing's 5.03% (StatCounter, May 2026).

Every browser ships with a default search engine baked in, and most people never touch that setting — which makes it one of the highest-value pieces of real estate Google owns. It's also proof that defaults only convert into share when the browser itself has real reach: Bing's search share has barely moved despite Edge shipping on every new Windows machine on the planet.

That's the mechanism the AI browsers are attacking directly. Comet and Atlas don't need to beat Chrome's install base to matter — they only need to intercept enough of those 'question moments' that would otherwise have opened a Google search tab. For the fuller picture of who currently owns that layer, see our breakdown of [search engine market share](/blog/search-engine-market-share). The short version: the query stream is still overwhelmingly Google's to lose, but this is the first time in over a decade that phrase has felt worth saying out loud.

## What brands should take from this

Answers are increasingly generated and delivered inside the browser itself, before a user ever lands on your website — which means being the source an AI engine trusts enough to cite matters as much as ranking on a results page ever did.
