## How Much Does SEO Cost?

Reported SEO pricing spans a wide range: roughly low hundreds monthly for DIY tools and self-managed work, low-to-mid four figures monthly for small-business retainers, and five figures monthly for enterprise programs. The real number depends on three things — how competitive your market is, how big your site is, and who's actually doing the work.

Before you get anywhere near a quote, you'll run into a louder question: is SEO even worth paying for anymore? We've [answered that one separately](/blog/is-seo-dead) — short version: no, AI answer engines changed what "ranking" means, they didn't end it. Assuming that hasn't scared you off, here's the pricing picture most people selling SEO won't give you first.

Reported bands, roughly, by who's doing the work:

- **DIY with tools only:** low hundreds monthly — software subscriptions, no labor cost.
- **Freelancer or small specialist:** low-to-mid four figures monthly.
- **Agency, competitive market:** mid-to-high four figures monthly.
- **Enterprise, multi-market program:** five figures monthly, sometimes well beyond it.

Every one of those is "commonly reported" — meaning it's what buyers say they paid, in surveys, forums, and comparison threads, not a published rate card. Nobody's actual SEO invoice is public. Treat any single number you read, including ours, as an orientation point, not a quote you can hold someone to.

## The Four Ways SEO Is Priced

SEO gets sold four ways: monthly retainer, project-based, hourly consulting, or performance-based. Retainers dominate because SEO is ongoing work, not a one-time fix — but the other three fit specific situations a retainer doesn't.

**Monthly retainer.** A fixed fee every month for ongoing work: content, technical fixes, link building, reporting. It fits anyone who needs SEO to keep compounding, which is most businesses. Red flag: a retainer with no defined deliverables list is a subscription to hope.

**Project-based.** A one-time fee for a defined scope — a technical audit, a site migration, a content cluster. It fits a business with one specific problem, not an ongoing need. Red flag: an "audit" that produces a long PDF and no prioritized list of what to actually fix.

**Hourly consulting.** Payment for a specialist's time, usually strategy or a second opinion rather than execution. It fits businesses that already have in-house marketing and need expert judgment, not more hands. Red flag: hourly billing on execution work you can't see — hours are easy to pad when nobody's watching the clock.

**Performance-based.** Payment tied to results — rankings hit, traffic gained, revenue attributed. It sounds safest and is actually the hardest to get right, because SEO results lag by months and attribution is always arguable. Red flag: any pitch that makes this sound risk-free. Someone still decides what counts as "performance," and it's rarely the buyer.

Here's how the four compare:

| Pricing model | Commonly reported band | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly retainer | Low four figures to five figures, monthly | Ongoing programs built to compound over time | Vague scope, "unlimited" everything |
| Project-based | Low-to-mid four figures per project; more for full overhauls | One-off fixes: audits, migrations, content sprints | Deliverables with no follow-through plan |
| Hourly consulting | Double-digit to low triple-digit rates, per hour | Strategy input, second opinions, in-house teams | Execution work billed by the hour |
| Performance-based | A cut of attributed value or a bonus tied to milestones | Buyers who can tightly define "success" upfront | Success metrics that are vague or move after signing |

## What Actually Drives the Price?

Two businesses in the same industry can get SEO quotes that differ by five times, and both quotes can be legitimate. Price tracks five variables, and any real quote should trace back to each one.

- **Market competitiveness.** Ranking a local plumber against three competitors costs nothing like ranking a SaaS company against a hundred funded startups for the same ten keywords.
- **Site size and technical state.** A clean 40-page site is a cheaper starting point than a 4,000-page site with a decade of redirect chains and orphaned pages.
- **Content volume.** Programs that need net-new content every month cost more than programs optimizing what already exists, because content production is the line item that scales directly with output.
- **Link difficulty.** Earning links in a niche with active journalists and bloggers costs less than a niche where nobody publishes anything worth linking to.
- **Reporting expectations.** Custom dashboards, weekly calls, and executive-ready summaries are real labor. Buyers who want heavy reporting are paying for a second, smaller job stacked on top of the SEO itself.

If a quote can't explain itself against these five, that's a red flag on its own — the price was set by what the market will bear, not by what your site actually needs.

## SEO Packages: Why Do Cheap Bundles Usually Disappoint?

Packages priced in the double digits a month are templated: the same technical checklist, the same handful of blog posts, the same generic outreach applied to every client regardless of market. They disappoint not because the work is fake — it usually gets done — but because it's identical to what your competitor bought.

Real SEO work requires judgment calls specific to your site: which keywords to prioritize, what content actually matches buyer intent, which technical fixes matter for your CMS. A templated package can't make those calls, because it's built to serve hundreds of accounts profitably at that price point. Someone is doing a fixed amount of "work" per client per month and calling it a strategy — the price is the tell.

Real work costs closer to what a skilled hire costs, because that's effectively what you're renting: a strategist's judgment, a writer's time, a developer's hours for technical fixes. When a quote is a fraction of what even a junior in-house marketer would cost, the arithmetic only works if the agency is spreading a template across many accounts instead of solving your problem specifically.

## How to Budget for SEO Sanely

Budget SEO like a channel, not a fixed expense — tie spend to what a customer is worth, not to what feels affordable this quarter. Early-stage businesses should spend less and expect a slower payback; established businesses with proven unit economics can spend more, because they already know what a ranking is worth.

Roughly, by stage:

- **Pre-product-market-fit:** keep spend low, favor DIY tools and a few hours of consulting over a full retainer. SEO compounds too slowly to be your main growth bet this early.
- **Early traction:** a small retainer or project-based engagement focused on your highest-intent pages, not broad coverage.
- **Scaling:** budget grows with content volume and link difficulty — this is usually when a full retainer earns its cost.
- **Enterprise or multi-market:** budget behaves more like a headcount decision than a marketing line item, because the program effectively needs a small team.

Whatever stage you're in, run the math before you sign anything. ## The Line Item Nobody Prices: AI Visibility

Almost no SEO quote, cheap or expensive, includes a line for whether AI answer engines actually recommend you. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, and Google's AI Overviews now answer a growing share of buyer questions directly, and traditional rankings-and-traffic reporting doesn't tell you if you show up inside those answers.

Rankings dashboards were built for blue links, not AI-generated answers. An agency can hit every KPI in your retainer — rankings up, traffic up — while your brand stays invisible in the answers AI gives the exact buyers you're trying to reach. That gap is real, not hypothetical, and almost nobody selling SEO checks for it before they take your money.

Some agencies have started [bundling AI-focused SEO services](/blog/ai-seo-services) into their retainers. It's worth understanding what that actually includes, and whether anyone is measuring it, before you pay extra for it.
