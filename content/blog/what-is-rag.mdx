RAG, short for retrieval-augmented generation, means an AI model looks up documents before it writes an answer. Grounding is the goal that lookup serves: an answer tied to sources it can point to. In AI search, retrieval decides who gets cited, because a page the engine never retrieves cannot be quoted. Our position: get retrieved first, then get quoted.

## What is RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)?

RAG is a technique where a language model retrieves relevant documents from an outside source and uses them as context while generating an answer, instead of relying only on training memory. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrieval-augmented_generation) describes it as letting a model retrieve and incorporate new information from external data sources. Our [glossary entry](/glossary/retrieval-augmented-generation) has the short version.

The term comes from [Lewis et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401), written at Facebook AI Research, University College London and New York University. They paired a model's built-in "parametric" memory with a "non-parametric" one: a vector index of Wikipedia, split into 100-word chunks, 21 million of them. Their RAG models generated more specific, diverse and factual text than a parametric-only baseline.

Note what was retrieved: passages, not whole pages. That detail, present since 2020, drives most of the advice below.

## What is grounding, and how is it different from RAG?

Grounding means connecting a model's output to verifiable sources of information, so each claim can be checked. [Google Cloud's documentation](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/grounding/overview) defines it that way and lists RAG as one route among several, alongside Google Search and Google Maps data. We use grounding for the outcome and RAG for the mechanism.

Vendors blur the two. Google's [Search guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) calls RAG a technique "also known as grounding" that retrieves pages from Google's Search index. Gemini's [API documentation](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/google-search) shows the loop: the model decides whether a Google Search can improve the answer, runs one or more queries, synthesizes the results and returns inline citations.

| | Ungrounded answer | Grounded answer |
|---|---|---|
| Draws on | Training memory, frozen at a cutoff | Sources fetched at question time |
| Citations | Nothing to point to | Links to the sources used |
| Freshness | Stops at the cutoff | As current as the index |
| Main risk | Confident invention | A wrong, stale or misread source |

The sources can be a company knowledge base, an uploaded document or the live web. AI search uses the live web, so your pages are candidates.

## How does RAG work, step by step?

RAG runs in two phases: an index is built ahead of time, then every question triggers retrieve, augment and generate. Google Cloud's [RAG Engine overview](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/rag-engine/rag-overview) lists the build stages as data ingestion, splitting into chunks, embedding and indexing. An embedding is a list of numbers that captures meaning, so related text sits close together.

At question time:

1. **Decide whether to search.** [OpenAI's documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/tools-web-search) says the model can choose to search or not. [Anthropic's](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/web-search-tool) says Claude searches for current or changing information and answers stable-knowledge questions directly.
2. **Fan out.** Google's AI features may run several related searches at once, which its guide calls query fan-out.
3. **Retrieve passages.** In [OpenAI's retrieval guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/retrieval), a sentence about the 1969 moon landing scores 65% semantic similarity to "When did we go to the moon?" with 0% keyword overlap. Names still matter: in the 2020 paper's ablation, plain keyword matching (BM25) did best on fact-checking claims, which the authors attribute to those claims being entity-centric.
4. **Rank and trim.** Only the top-scoring passages enter the prompt; OpenAI exposes a score threshold that drops weaker chunks.
5. **Generate and cite.** The model writes from those passages. OpenAI's web search returns inline citations by default.

## Why does retrieval decide who gets cited?

A model can only cite what reached its context, so your page must pass three gates: crawled and indexed, retrieved for the question, and chosen for the answer. OpenAI's documentation shows the funnel: its `sources` field lists every URL the model consulted, and it notes the number of sources is often greater than the number of citations.

The first gate has explicit rules. For Google, a page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet, and the site must be included in Search generative AI features in Search Console, per the [Search guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide). For ChatGPT, [OpenAI says](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots) sites opted out of OAI-SearchBot will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers. Retrieval is ranking with far fewer slots; [what AI search is](/blog/what-is-ai-search) covers the shift, and [how ChatGPT chooses sources](/answers/how-does-chatgpt-choose-sources) covers the selection.

Here is what we found about access. In [AEOeye's crawler access study](/blog/ai-crawler-user-agent-directory) (fetched 3 October 2026, 384 sites with a usable robots.txt), 27 of 38 news publishers (71.1%) blocked at least one AI retrieval bot, against 1 of 193 SaaS sites (0.5%). Per-site data is in the [CSV](/resources/ai-crawler-access-study-2026-10.csv). It is a convenience sample, one fetch per site, and robots.txt is a request, not enforcement.

The reading we stand behind: the publishers AI engines might lean on are often fenced off, while brand sites rarely block anything. Your risk is seldom a robots.txt rule. It is being absent from the pages that do get retrieved.

## How do you make content retrievable and quotable?

Be retrievable first: crawlable, indexed, current. Then be quotable: every paragraph names its subject, states one fact and cites its source. Do not "chunk for AI". Google's guide says there is no requirement to break content into tiny pieces, and that Google Search ignores llms.txt.

But pipelines split text anyway, wherever they choose. OpenAI's file search defaults to 800-token chunks with 400 tokens of overlap, and you control none of the cut points.

Anthropic's [contextual retrieval post](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval) shows the failure: a chunk reading "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter" names neither company nor period. Adding context cut failed retrievals by 35%, and by 49% combined with keyword matching. Builders patch this in their index. You can patch it at the source.

- **Allow the retrieval crawlers.** Check robots.txt and firewall rules for OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot and Claude-SearchBot; Perplexity's [docs](https://docs.perplexity.ai/guides/bots) recommend allowing PerplexityBot and note that Perplexity-User generally ignores robots.txt. Our [robots.txt generator](/tools/ai-robots-txt-generator) writes the rules.
- **Name the entity every time.** Write "AEOeye", not "the tool", so a lifted passage still says who it is about.
- **Lead with the answer, then the evidence.** One fact per paragraph, with a number, date and source. In the GEO paper's benchmark, adding [citations, quotations and statistics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735) gave the top gains, 30-40% relative on position-adjusted word count, varying by domain. See [what makes content quotable](/answers/what-makes-content-quotable-by-ai).
- **Say something original.** Google's guide says its AI systems look at a variety of sources, so a unique viewpoint helps.
- **Keep dates true.** Claude's search results include a `page_age` field, described as when the site was last updated, so make your update dates accurate.
- **Resist near-duplicate pages.** Google warns that variants made to manipulate AI responses violate its scaled content abuse policy. On 3 October 2026 we merged 165 of our own pages into 42 (85 into 37 canonical pages, 80 into 5 hubs). We believe one strong page per question beats several thin ones, but we have not measured the effect.

## What are RAG's limits?

RAG makes answers checkable, not correct. Four failure points matter for anyone hoping to be cited: stale indexes, bad sources, unsupported citations and wrong links.

- **Stale index.** The 2020 paper swapped in a two-year-older Wikipedia index and asked "Who is {position}?" for 82 world leaders who had changed. Accuracy was 68% with the matching 2018 index and 4% with the stale 2016 one. Google says crawling can take [a few days to a few weeks](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/ask-google-to-recrawl) after a recrawl request.
- **Bad sources in, bad answers out.** After odd AI Overviews in 2024, Google [explained](https://blog.google/products/search/ai-overviews-update-may-2024/) that satirical content republished on a software provider's site, and sarcastic forum posts, had been surfaced.
- **Retrieved is not supported.** A [2023 audit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848) of four generative search engines found only 51.5% of sentences fully supported by their citations and 74.5% of citations supporting their sentence. Those products have changed since. Relatedly, [models use information in long contexts unevenly](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172), with relevant text in the middle often handled worst.
- **Wrong or fabricated links.** In a March 2025 [Tow Center test](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php), eight AI search tools were asked to identify the source of news excerpts and answered incorrectly on more than 60% of 1,600 queries. USA Today blocks ChatGPT's crawler, yet ChatGPT cited a syndicated Yahoo News copy.
