## What is anchor text?

Anchor text is the clickable, visible text of a hyperlink — the words a reader clicks to move from one page to another. It's usually styled in a different color or underlined, though the visual treatment varies by site. Search engines read that text as a label: it's a plain-language hint about what the linked page contains.

The mechanics are simple. Anchor text lives inside a hyperlink's HTML markup, sitting between the opening and closing tags of a link. That phrase — the words between the tags — is what a visitor sees and clicks, and it's also what a crawler indexes as context for the destination URL.

Change the words inside the link, and you change the signal, even if the destination stays exactly the same. That's what separates anchor text from ordinary body copy — it's simultaneously navigation, description, and endorsement, all packed into a few words.

## Why anchor text matters

Anchor text matters because it's one of the clearest relevance signals available for understanding what a page is about — and it comes from outside the page itself. Google has used anchor text as a ranking factor since its original PageRank research, precisely because a link's wording reflects how someone else independently describes a destination, which is harder to fake than on-page copy.

The signal works on two levels:

- **For readers**, descriptive anchor text sets expectations before the click. "Download the 2024 pricing guide" tells someone exactly what's waiting; "click here" tells them nothing and forces a guess.
- **For search engines**, anchor text functions like a crowdsourced summary. A page linked from many independent sources using the phrase "email marketing software" accumulates real evidence that it's actually about email marketing software — evidence the site owner didn't write themselves.

This is also why your [backlink](/blog/what-is-a-backlink) profile matters beyond raw link count. The exact words other sites choose when linking to you shape how search engines classify your page, sometimes more decisively than your own headlines and body copy do. That's true whether the link comes from someone else's site or from another page on your own — which is why internal anchor text deserves just as much intentional care as anything you'd pursue through outreach.

## Types of anchor text

There are five common anchor text types, and each carries a different level of optimization risk. Exact-match anchors repeat your target keyword word-for-word; branded anchors use your company or product name; naked URLs are just the raw link pasted as visible text.

| Anchor Type | Example | When to Use | Over-Optimization Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact-match | "email marketing software" | Sparingly, on high-authority, highly relevant links | High — pattern reads as manipulation |
| Partial match | "best email marketing tools" | Naturally, when the phrase fits the sentence | Low to medium |
| Branded | "AEOeye" or "Mailchimp" | Most external links back to your homepage or brand pages | Very low |
| Generic | "click here," "read more," "this article" | Low-value contextual links, not target keywords | Very low, but a weak signal |
| Naked URL | "https://example.com/blog/post" | Citations, references, forum and comment links | Very low |

No single type is inherently good or bad on its own. The risk lives in the distribution across your whole link profile — a natural, healthy mix leans toward branded and naked URLs, with exact-match kept to a deliberate minority.

## Anchor text best practices

The best anchor text describes the destination in language a reader would actually say out loud, varies naturally across mentions, and never forces a keyword where it doesn't belong. If a phrase sounds stiff read aloud, it will read as stiff — and as manipulative — to a search engine too.

A handful of rules hold up across most sites:

1. **Write for the sentence first.** The anchor should fit naturally into the surrounding copy, not look like a keyword dropped in from a spreadsheet.
2. **Vary your phrasing.** Link to the same destination with different anchor text across different mentions — partial matches, related phrases, branded variations — instead of repeating one exact string.
3. **Match the anchor to the destination.** The text should accurately preview what's on the other end; mismatches frustrate readers and send mixed signals to crawlers.
4. **Don't neglect internal links.** Internal anchor text is a core part of [on-page SEO](/blog/what-is-on-page-seo) — it's one of the few relevance signals you control completely, unlike backlinks earned from other sites.
5. **Never outsource this to a template.** Link-building services that insert identical exact-match phrases across dozens of placements are the fastest route to a red-flag profile, a risk worth understanding fully before running any [link building](/blog/what-is-link-building) campaign.

## The over-optimization trap

Over-optimization happens when too many links to the same page use identical exact-match anchor text — a pattern that organic, independently-earned links almost never produce. Search engines read that uniformity as a sign of manipulation, not relevance.

The logic is straightforward: if fifteen different websites all happened to link to your page with the exact same six-word phrase, that isn't how people naturally write. Genuine link profiles are messy by nature. People paraphrase, use your brand name, paste a raw URL, or write "read more" without thinking twice. A profile that's 80% exact-match on a single keyword looks engineered because, functionally, it is.

This risk concentrates in [off-page SEO](/blog/what-is-off-page-seo), where your anchor text profile is aggregated across every external site linking to you — a profile you don't fully control but should actively monitor. If a spike of exact-match anchors turns up (from an old link-building push, a competitor's negative SEO attempt, or a habit worth retiring), the standard fix isn't removal — it's diversifying new links going forward until the ratio rebalances naturally.

## Anchor text in the AI era

Anchor text now serves two audiences at once: it's still a relevance signal for traditional search rankings, and it's increasingly evidence AI systems use to map relationships between entities and topics. When ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google's AI Overviews decide what to cite in an answer, they're weighing which sources are consistently and clearly associated with a subject — and anchor text is part of that evidence trail. That's a meaningfully different bar than counting keyword matches, and it rewards sites that have been described the same accurate way for years, not just sites with the most links.

Here's the shift worth internalizing. Classic SEO treated anchor text mainly as a keyword-matching exercise: does this anchor contain the target phrase, yes or no. AI answer engines weigh something closer to consistency and clarity — is this page described in recognizably similar language across many independent, unconnected mentions? That consistency is what builds entity clarity: a stable, unambiguous association between your brand and the topic it's known for.

A few practical implications follow:

- **Descriptive beats keyword-stuffed.** An anchor like "AEOeye's AI visibility audit" teaches both search engines and language models what the entity actually is, not just which phrase it should rank for.
- **Consistency reinforces identity.** Varied but conceptually aligned anchor language — "AI search visibility checker," "AEOeye audit tool," "AI citation tracker" — strengthens a coherent entity profile instead of diluting it the way scattered, unrelated phrasing would.
- **Pair anchors with structured data.** Anchor text tells a crawler what a link means in prose; schema markup states it explicitly in code. Using both together gives AI systems redundant, machine-readable confirmation of what a page is and how it connects to everything else on your site.

The practical takeaway: anchor text discipline isn't just an old ranking-algorithm nicety. It's part of how you build a legible, citable identity across every system — human and AI — currently trying to work out what your site is actually about.
