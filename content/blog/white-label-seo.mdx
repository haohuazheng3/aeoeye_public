Search "white label SEO" and the first page reads like a trade show floor — a dozen vendors, each telling you their white label program is the trustworthy one. That's not an accident; the buyers researching this term are agency owners deciding whether to resell fulfillment, and the sellers know it. This guide isn't pitching a program. It's the conversation an experienced agency owner would have with you before you sign anything.

## What is white label SEO?

White label SEO is an arrangement where an agency resells another company's SEO fulfillment under its own brand. Your client sees your logo, your account manager, and your reports; a separate vendor does the actual work — link building, content production, technical fixes — behind the scenes.

The client relationship stays yours. So does the invoice, the strategy calls, and the blame when something goes wrong. The vendor's job ends at delivering the work; managing what that work does to a real client relationship is still on you.

This is different from a referral or affiliate arrangement, where you hand the client off entirely and collect a commission. In white label, you never let go of the account. That distinction matters more than most pitch decks admit, because it decides who the client calls when rankings don't move.

## Why agencies use it

Agencies use white label SEO to add a service line and revenue without hiring specialists, building an in-house process, or buying an expensive tool stack. You sell the outcome; the vendor builds it; you keep the spread between what you charge and what you pay.

That's a legitimate, common model — not a shortcut for agencies that can't be bothered to do real work. Three reasons it holds up:

- **Scale without hiring.** You can take on ten new SEO clients this quarter without recruiting, onboarding, or training a single link builder or content writer.
- **Offer SEO without owning the expertise.** A web design shop or PR agency can say yes to an SEO request instead of losing the account to a competitor who can.
- **Margin on fulfillment, not headcount.** Your cost scales with client volume instead of salary, which is a more forgiving cash flow shape for a small agency.

Some agencies stretch the same logic to newer categories, bundling in [AI-related SEO services](/blog/ai-seo-services) before they've defined internally what that even means to a client. That's worth doing carefully — more on why later.

## The risks nobody's pitch deck includes

The real risk in white label SEO isn't that the model is dishonest. It's that you can't see quality until a client's results stall six months in, and by then the damage is attached to your brand, not the vendor's.

Four risks worth sitting with before you sign a wholesale agreement:

- **Quality variance you can't see until churn.** The demo deliverable and the ongoing deliverable are not always the same standard. You often only find out at renewal, when the client is already gone.
- **Templated work across many resellers' clients.** The same vendor may be fulfilling for forty different agencies. Templated content and formulaic link patterns are how they keep margins at that scale — and it shows to anyone paying attention, including Google.
- **Communication lag.** Feedback travels from client to you, from you to the vendor, and back again the same way. Every fix takes longer than it would in-house, and nuance gets lost along the relay.
- **Your brand absorbs their mistakes.** If the vendor builds spammy links or writes thin content, the client doesn't fire the vendor. They fire you.

The uncomfortable summary: your contract is with the client, but their outcome is decided by a team you've never met, that you don't manage, running a process you can't see. That's the entire risk in one sentence.

## How to vet a white label provider

Vetting a white label provider means asking the questions their sales call is built to avoid: who actually touches the work, what a real deliverable looks like for a business like your client's, and what happens the month nothing moves.

Ask for these specifically, before you sign anything:

- A real sample deliverable for a niche close to your client's — not a generic template with the logo swapped.
- Whether the work is done in-house or re-outsourced further down the chain (yes, that happens — a "white label" vendor subcontracting to another white label vendor).
- Full access to raw reporting data, not just a branded dashboard with a proprietary "authority score."
- A specific answer for what happens when a client's rankings stall for two straight quarters.
- Exit terms: what you own if you leave, and how much notice the contract requires.

| Vetting Dimension | Green Flag | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Sample deliverables | Real, niche-specific work, client names redacted | Generic templates or stock screenshots only |
| Who does the work | Named in-house team, you can talk to a strategist | Vague on staffing, work re-outsourced further offshore |
| Reporting transparency | Raw, exportable data, no invented metrics | Vanity dashboard with an unexplained "authority score" |
| Response when results stall | Documented diagnostic and escalation process | "Algorithm update" as the answer, every time |
| Contract exit terms | Month-to-month or short notice, you keep the work product | Long lock-in, content and links not owned by you on exit |

If a provider hesitates on more than one of these, that's your answer.

## White label pricing: the economics

White label SEO pricing runs on a spread: you pay the vendor a wholesale fulfillment fee per client per month, mark it up to your retail price, and keep the difference. The model is genuinely profitable — but the cheapest wholesale tiers buy you the cheapest labor the vendor has, and that shows up in the work.

What moves the wholesale price:

- The client's site size, authority, and how competitive their market is.
- Deliverable volume — how many links, how much content, how many technical hours are actually included versus implied.
- How much account management the vendor provides you versus leaving you to translate technical updates into client language yourself.

Most agencies price retail as a flat retainer regardless of what the vendor actually costs that month, which is reasonable — clients want predictability. The trap is on the wholesale side: racing a vendor's price down compresses quality first, long before it compresses your margin. ## The AI-era gap in white label offerings

Most white label packages still report success in rankings and organic traffic alone. Clients are increasingly asking a different question — does ChatGPT recommend us — and most reseller catalogs don't have an answer built in yet.

That's a real gap, not a hypothetical one. Buyers are starting research in ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overviews before they ever run a traditional search. An agency reselling rankings-only SEO is optimizing for a smaller share of that behavior every quarter. Agencies building out [generative engine optimization as a service line](/blog/geo-agency) are ahead of that shift; agencies still selling only backlink counts are behind it and don't know it yet.

This is where an audit — not another reseller relationship — is genuinely more useful. An agency can run one before a pitch to show a prospect where they stand today, or attach it to an existing white label retainer as a quarterly checkpoint that a rankings report alone can't produce. If you're not sure how to even define that measurement, [how to measure AI visibility](/blog/measuring-ai-visibility) is the place to start before you promise it to a client.

## Verdict

White label SEO works for agencies that vet providers hard and stay genuinely involved in the client relationship. It fails as a set-and-forget margin play where you collect the spread and never look at the work again.

The agencies that do well with this model treat the vendor like a real subcontractor: they read the raw data, sit in on strategy calls, and re-check sample work every year, not just at signup. The agencies that get burned are the ones who signed up over a Slack DM, forwarded client logins, and found out something was wrong only when a client's cancellation email arrived.

If you're going to resell SEO, resell it like your name is actually on it — because it is.
